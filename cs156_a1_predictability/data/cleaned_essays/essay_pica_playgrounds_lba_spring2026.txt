Our group will explore pica in children in Tokyo and how public play spaces can be designed to reduce risk and better support children who may engage in pica, especially those with autism spectrum disorder (ASD) or other developmental disabilities. Pica involves the repeated ingestion of non-food items such as paper, soil, or small objects, and it can lead to serious harms, including choking, poisoning, infection, and gastrointestinal obstruction (Al Nasser et al., 2023). In Tokyo, this makes playgrounds an important setting for prevention, as urban public play spaces often feature shared equipment, sensory stimulation, and frequent contact with loose or portable materials. At the same time, Tokyo is already investing in more inclusive park design: Tokyo’s accessible tourism materials note the installation of inclusive playground equipment intended for children with and without disabilities to play together, and local examples such as Toshima Kids Park explicitly describe themselves as part of an inclusive park effort (Tokyo Metropolitan Government, n.d.). Our proposal will therefore focus on PICA-friendly playgrounds in Tokyo as an environmental intervention. We will investigate how design choices such as safer surfacing, reduced access to ingestible hazards, clear sightlines for caregivers, secure boundaries, and sensory-rich but non-ingestible play options may lower risk while preserving inclusive play. This is especially relevant because pica is more common in children with ASD and other developmental disabilities than in the general population (Fields et al., 2021).

This source gives a clear clinical overview of pica, including diagnostic criteria, common presentations, possible causes, and medical risks. It explains that pica is defined in DSM-5 as eating non-food, non-nutritive substances for at least one month, and that diagnosis depends on developmental appropriateness and cultural context. It also summarizes major etiological theories, including links with stress, neglect, and micronutrient deficiency, while noting that no single cause fully explains the condition. This helps us define the disorder accurately before moving into design implications. It also highlights the diversity of substances that may be ingested, which matters in playground planning: if children with pica may ingest dirt, paper, powder, or other loose materials, then environmental design should minimize access to small or hazardous ingestible items. Because this is a medical reference source rather than a narrow single-study article, it is especially useful for grounding the assignment’s basic definitions and risks before we build an intervention argument.

This is more of a review paper. It gathers evidence on how inclusive playgrounds should be designed for children with disabilities. It then synthesizes findings from 35 peer-reviewed studies and identifies 13 evidence-informed recommendations, along with one promising practice. It expands the discussion beyond equipment alone and considers entry points, surfacing, paths, built surroundings, social environment, trained staff, and family involvement in design. One especially useful point is that enclosing a playground may help prevent children from straying, particularly when hazards such as traffic or water are nearby. Although the review is not specifically about pica, it gives strong evidence base for the design side of our proposal. We can adapt its principles to pica-friendly design by arguing for barrier-free access, safer materials, easier supervision, hazard reduction, and consultation with families who understand a child’s behavioral risks. This source, therefore, helps connect our clinical topic to a practical environmental intervention.

A major challenge in pica research is that the behavior is influenced by different factors in humans (e.g., driven by micronutrient deficiency, sensory seeking, compulsive behavior, or environmental access), so scientists often study specific components of pica using different model systems rather than expecting one perfect “pica model” (Al Nasser et al., 2023). In animal research, the most established approach is the rodent kaolin-consumption model, where rats given access to kaolin clay increase kaolin intake after exposure to nausea- or toxin-inducing manipulations (Takeda et al., 1993). Because rats cannot vomit, kaolin intake is widely used as a proxy for nausea and has been used to test pharmacological and neural mechanisms linked to sickness behavior (Takeda et al., 1993; Horn et al., 2009). For example, cisplatin (a chemotherapy agent) increases kaolin consumption, and kaolin access can reduce weight loss and illness severity, suggesting the behavior may be partly adaptive in that context (De Jonghe et al., 2009). Stress can also modulate kaolin intake in these models, highlighting how internal state changes may interact with ingestion of non-food substances (Guo et al., 2022). A second line of work examines iron interactions, using controlled designs to test whether clay ingestion changes iron bioavailability or absorption. This is useful because iron deficiency and pica are strongly linked clinically (Ganesan & Vasauskas, 2023). These models help clarify whether “pica-like” ingestion is a cause or consequence of nutrient deficiency under specific conditions. However, these approaches have key limitations for predicting real-world intervention outcomes (like PICA-friendly playground design). First, kaolin models primarily capture illness-related ingestion (a nausea proxy), not the more developmentally and clinically relevant patterns seen in children, especially those with ASD, where pica may be maintained by sensory reinforcement or repetitive behavior (Fields et al., 2021). Second, animals consume standardized materials under tightly controlled lab conditions, whereas playground risk depends on diverse objects, supervision, and environmental opportunity, making direct translation uncertain. Therefore, the best confidence in predicting intervention impact comes from triangulation: animal models (mechanisms), clinical human studies (who is at risk), and behavioral assessment in real settings (what maintains the behavior). For our proposal, animal research is most useful for assessing plausibility (e.g., internal-state drivers), but playground design decisions should be grounded primarily in human prevalence and behavioral-function evidence.
