The San Francisco Food Security Task Force report of 2023 notes that 30% of children whose families’ income is $130000 does not guarantee a meal at the table (Food Security Task Force Reports and Assessments | San Francisco, 2022.). This is due to the high cost of living in the Bay Area with the cost of living index of San Francisco being at 169.9% that of the national average and incessant inflation as the aftershocks of COVID continue to rumble. A part of the same metric used to measure the high cost of living is high food costs. San Francisco’s food prices are 31% more than the national average (Winters, 2023). The government tries to alleviate this burden by giving out food stamps. However, we see California ranks 46th in the country in food stamp utilization. Food insecurity, I believe, can be divided into two major factors: costs and availability. We see costs of food increasing since the pandemic due to the inflation according to Winters. The mechanics of demand and supply are too complex and further resource allocation in this issue is unlikely to have any impact. We can further group availability into whether the food is organic or inorganic. We will consider organic food as the most affected part of the population and children need nutritious foods for growth. Being able to provide organic food to homesteads with children would, therefore, have the most impact on food insecurity in San Francisco. San Francisco has numerous farmers’ markets and big supermarkets which provide organic food. Organic food poses a unique problem in that it fluctuates from seasons. A season with liked produce would increase demand and seasons without would chase businesses away. However, their distribution is uneven. A deeper look unearths “food deserts” spread over the city. The United States Department of Agriculture defines a food desert as a place where at least one-third of the area's population is living more than a half-mile from the closest supermarket or large grocery store in urban areas. The designation and specific boundaries of a food desert can help us alleviate this problem. Furthermore, these zones contain the bulk of people suffering from food insecurity. San Francisco has 171 such designations with the biggest clusters being the ones in Bayview-Hunters point and Tenderloin districts (“Food Deserts”: Nearly 900 Neighborhoods Across Bay Area Have Limited Access to Food, 2021). These two districts, however, face different challenges. The Bayview only has two big stores and has a lot of space for improvement while the Tenderloin is dense lacking space for more stores (Graf, 2022). These would be the obstacles we face as we address the matter in these two districts. Our task, therefore, would be to try to bridge the organic food gap existing in these designated areas.
