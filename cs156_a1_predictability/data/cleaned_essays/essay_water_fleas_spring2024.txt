Urbanization has led to increased stress on waterbodies. To ensure these water systems are pure, we pour pesticides to eliminate the natives we describe as contaminants (Muhammad Syafrudin et al., 2021). A victim of such exposure is Daphnia magna (more commonly known as water fleas). One study noted that exposure to pesticides rich in chlorpyrifos, on average, reduced the survival of water fleas. However, the same study noted that the more exposed urban water fleas had a higher chance of survival than rural unexposed ones (Brans et al., 2021). This leads to our hypothesis: exposed water flea populations have evolved increased resistance to chemical agents compared to rural populations due to prolonged exposure to pesticides leading to selective pressure favoring individuals with genetic traits that are more resistant to chlorpyrifos. A prediction from this would be that urban water flea populations have higher survival rates than rural water fleas when placed in standardized chlorpyrifos mixtures. An alternative causal mechanism to explain the resistance can be seen from Vandegehuchte et al. (2010) who note that urban water fleas have adapted to feed on chemical agents in pesticides as they have an increased number of an enzyme family (cytochrome P450) involved in breaking down these chemical agents. A prediction from this would be that urban flea populations have included in their feeding behavior the ability to consume chemical agents in pesticides. Another prediction is that we expect a positive correlation between concentrations of P450 enzymes and the concentration of pesticide contamination. Lastly, we expect urban fleas with higher expression of cytochrome P450 to have higher survival rates than their rural counterparts when exposed to standardized pesticide concentrations. The second prediction seems more testable, as we can create many groups based on different pesticide contamination levels and observe a relationship between these contamination levels and P450 buildup in water fleas. The generational period of a water flea is about a week and it produces asexually (Ebert, 2024). Given that about 500 generations seem to be settled on a heuristic to measure experimentally driven evolution harvardgazette (2014) this will make our experiment either last 9 years if we opt for an interventional study or we would need to find complete data focused on P450 and pesticide levels. Given the sensitive nature of water systems: seasonal fluctuations in temperature affect water flea reproduction Ebert (2024), there’s also fluctuations in pesticide concentration due to different agricultural demands affecting the seepage of fertilizers into freshwater systems Gilliom et.al (2006) which might affect adaptability of water fleas and our ability to determine positive correlation in our experiment, and water acidity or alkalinity which might alter the chemical properties of pesticides introducing another variability reducing our ability to determine the correlation. Therefore, an interventional study would be appropriate. This also gives us a chance to make another prediction with regard to P450: for each group exposed to pesticides, we expect an overall increase in P450 expression as the number of generations increases. To find samples using simple random sampling, we would be collecting water fleas in unexposed freshwater systems in California’s Central Valley. We can obtain a sample frame of the waterbodies in this area from the U.S Environmental Protection Agency and a list of waterbodies unexposed to pesticides in Central Valley was formed by the Department of Pesticide Regulation (Pesticides and Water | Californians for Pesticide Reform, 2024). From this list, we can randomly select a site to collect our water fleas. This is because of environmental concerns of disrupting habitats and, as evidenced by previous studies that exploit water fleas’ asexuality by having just one flea from the onset that is cultured to produce 200 neonates for each group Barata et.al (2004). While this may expose us to confounding variables on the influence of unique waterbody ecosystems that might influence generalizability, we are assuming a level of homogeneity on the ecosystems in such a relatively small geographical region. In the site, we would employ systematic sampling. Given the method to collect water fleas by Aliferis (2020), the nets thrown into the water to trap the water fleas can be zoned according to depth. Therefore, we can make 10 zones from which we can select a number between 1-5 and take its multiple. We would then collect 20 water fleas each from these two zones. After collecting our samples, we would return all unselected critters in the water bodies. This increases our chances of getting representative samples and reduces the impact of extraneous variables associated with depth that might impact our correlation analysis. However, the same pattern can render our sample biased if there is an extraneous variable that affects the preferred depth of water fleas. We would then perform stratified sampling, Sibbald & Roland (1994) on the 40 water fleas based on age using von Bertalanffy's function into juvenile, adolescent, and senior, Geller (1987). We are using stratified sampling to select juvenile samples as they are less exposed to environmental stressors. In the event they’re more than one, we select one of them randomly using simple random sampling. After culturing, we can conduct a randomized controlled trial, Sibbald & Roland (1994) on the collected water flea samples. We would first divide them into two main groups: control and treatment. In the control group, water fleas would not be exposed to chlorpyrifos. We could have used the unexposed waterbodies as a natural history group but given the longevity of the experiment, we chose to include our own control group. In the exposed groups, we would have three levels of chlorpyrifos concentrations. Based on the acute toxicity data on water fleas, appropriate concentration levels for chlorpyrifos for the comparison groups can be determined as follows: a low concentration range between 0.5 μg/L and 1.0 μg/L, a medium concentration range between 1.0 μg/L and 1.5 μg/L, and a high concentration range between 1.5 μg/L and 2.0 μg/L (Maggio et al., 2021). These concentrations would also include “normal” food but negatively correlated to pesticide levels such that we are not starving the water fleas. They would also be kept at room temperature with maintained pH and oxygen levels which affects the activity of the water fleas (Ebert, 2024). To confirm if P450 is the main factor in pesticide metabolism, we can have another group containing an inhibitor like silver particles (Qiao et al., 2022). We would measure the expression levels of cytochrome P450 in all groups at regular intervals (10 generations) throughout the experiment. Given our unlimited budget, we can accurately measure P450 expression using a technique called quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) (Wu et al., 2019). Firstly, we would measure from each treatment group whether there is an increase in P450 expression after each 10th generation. Secondly, we would measure if there is a positive correlation between the expression of P450 and pesticide concentration. Our tests are practical in that they can refute the hypothesis. The first one can refute the hypothesis when the fleas can ingest more pesticide while P450 stays constant or reduces. The second can refute the hypothesis if we notice that the expression of P450 does not correlate with increasing pesticide concentrations. This idealized condition might make our results hard to generalize due to the complexities of water systems. It is also time-intensive to maintain the concentration levels of the groups. However, an RCT gives us more confidence to declare associations between our pesticide concentrations and P450 expression. In our study, we expect to observe an increase in cytochrome P450 expression levels in water flea populations exposed to pesticides as the generations increase. This would support the hypothesis of evolutionary adaptation to pesticide exposure by increased expression of P450. Additionally, we anticipate a positive correlation between P450 expression and pesticide concentration, confirming the role of P450 enzymes in pesticide detoxification within water flea populations. These results would provide critical insights into the mechanisms of pesticide resistance and adaptive evolution in freshwater organisms.
