The San Francisco Food Security Task Force report of 2023 notes that 30% of children whose families’ income is $130000 does not guarantee a meal at the table (Food Security Task Force Reports and Assessments | San Francisco, 2022). As CNBC notes, this is due to the high cost of living in the Bay Area with the cost of living index of San Francisco being at 169.9% that of the national average and incessant inflation as the aftershocks of COVID continue to rumble. A part of the same metric used to measure the high cost of living is high food costs, with San Francisco’s food prices being 31% more than the national average (Winters, 2023). This set of such unconducive conditions leave 1 in 4 of all San Franciscans facing food insecurity (Food Security Task Force Reports and Assessments | San Francisco, 2022). Food insecurity, I believe, can be divided into two major factors: costs and availability. We see costs of food increasing since the pandemic due to the inflation according to Winters. The mechanics of demand and supply are too complex and further resource allocation on this issue is unlikely to have any impact from such a small organization. We can further group availability into whether the food is organic or fast food. We will consider organic food as the most affected part of the population and families prioritize nutritious foods for growth. Being able to provide organic food to homesteads with children would, therefore, have the most impact on food insecurity in San Francisco. Organic food poses problems: cost, availability, and their shelf life. Organic food is usually costly in terms of money, as it is more expensive than fast food (Haspel, 2023). In this district where a third of people earn less than $15000 and spend half their income on rent, quantity reigns over quality (Scaling up: Ensuring Tenderloin Parks Are Safe and Active, n.d.). In addition, their short lifespan poses a limit on how much a resident can take and store. Most residents in San Francisco lack cooling facilities in their houses making it infeasible to get healthier but perishable organic food (Food Security and Services in San Francisco's Tenderloin, 2013). In our case, we will focus on availability given the density of the district. San Francisco has numerous farmers’ markets and big supermarkets which provide organic food. However, their distribution is uneven. A deeper look at the distribution of these stores unearths “food deserts” spread over the city. A food desert is an area where at least one-third of the area's population is living more than a half-mile from the closest supermarket or large grocery store in an urban area (USDA ERS - Documentation, 2015). The designation and specific boundaries of a food desert can help us see which areas don’t have access to organic food. San Francisco has 171 such designations with the biggest clusters being the ones in Bayview-Hunters point and Tenderloin districts, with the latter being denser (“Food Deserts”: Nearly 900 Neighborhoods Across Bay Area Have Limited Access to Food, 2021). Data discloses that a third of people in the Tenderloin earn less than $15000 a year, that is more than 10000 of the 32000 in the district spread in just 40 blocks (Scaling up: Ensuring Tenderloin Parks Are Safe and Active, n.d.). If the San Francisco Food Task Force finding is anything to go by, if a family earning $130000 finds it difficult to afford food, then the situation is dire in the district. We can focus the organization to focus on these blocks, even to succeed in 5 would mean ensuring food security in 1250 people (assuming an even distribution of the people facing food insecurity in the 40 blocks). To succeed would mean dealing with the ‘competition’ as Tenderloin has the highest density of fast food stores in the city (Food Security and Services in San Francisco's Tenderloin, 2013) dealing with the stigma that comes from being seen taking ‘free’ food (Costanzo, 2022), and finding alternative ways to setting up shop in a densely populated area. To better understand the situation, I volunteered with the SF-Marina Food bank in a pop-up pantry. Pop-ups like these try to reduce food insecurity by handing out fresh produce to registered locals weekly. One of fellow volunteers was a 79 year old and has been doing this for decades told me that this gesture is immeasurable to the families and helps some 55000 families within and in the greater Bay Area every week (San Francisco-Marin Food Bank - Every $1 Donated Provides 2 Meals, 2023). However, they don’t fully solve the problem. The centralized food bank, though providing the locals with food, are not tailored to the community. The pack of burritos I was handing out and the pack of tortilla chips, for example, were often snubbed. This pointed to an underlying problem: insensitivity of the food bank towards residents’ tastes. In addition, the food banks are downsizing with reduced funding from the government. To keep afloat, some don’t accept new applications and some limit only one family member to enroll as reported by Jones (2023). This would only expose families already registered and those wanting to register to the pangs of hunger. The county offers other options. CalFresh, which is California’s version of food stamps, is available to eligible families on the basis of their income. These coupons can be cashed at eligible shops and farmer’s markets. However, The Imprint notes that California’s usage of food stamps is the fifth lowest in the nation. This is attributed to the tedious reverification process done every six months which means that of those who drop out of the program, 75% are still eligible (Tiano & Abram, 2021). Our task, therefore, is to strike a balance between cost and attractability of food items offered to our registered members. To do this, I would suggest a gradual shift in the source of our food starting with a single block. We should look toward community supported agriculture programs (CSA): where a community and a farmer become mutual partners, “with the growers and consumers providing mutual support and sharing the risks and benefits of food production” (Community Supported Agriculture | National Agricultural Library, 2017). The same article highlights how most food can be delivered bi-weekly, which will not impose a storage burden on our residents. The variety on offer by these CSAs is available online and through a survey on our residents, we can take note of their fancies and approach CSAs which cater for these likings one at a time. By noting the reactions of our constituents, we can respond by either increasing or decreasing the amount of produce by a particular CSA to reduce waste. In terms of costs, the California government allows CSAs to use food stamps with a simple application (Apply to Accept CalFresh for Your CSA And/or Farm Stand! Increase Access to Fresh Produce in Your Community ~ Expand Your Customer Base Ỹ, n.d.). Upon approval, our residents should be able to get better food without pinching their income. In addition, we should strive to avoid reverification processes as long as the food stamps are valid. Our goal is, afterall, to provide food to the residents. By leveraging CSAs, we can provide in demand fresh produce while mitigating storage challenges.
