Though blessed with many prized institutions of higher learning, the graduation rate in the United States is just 62% (Completing College | National Student Clearinghouse Research Center, 2022). In this paper, I will try to understand what explains student persistence in institutions of higher education. Stewart (2015) notes that the most important time period for a college student is their freshman year. Stewart highlighted factors such as demographics, socioeconomic status, academic performance, and support structures which we shall try to understand their role in student retention. A Google search for most colleges in the United States comes with two statistics in the right corner: acceptance rate and graduation rate. The U.S News ranking contains a list of this and a few buckets emerge (At These National Universities, Freshmen Are Most Likely to Return, 2023). First, we see a pot of highly selective institutions with high retention rates. Graduated students are mostly in graduate schools or boast of the highest income in the country. The schools shield incoming students from financial woes as the alumni proudly donate and are reciprocated of their kindness by preference in freshman admissions. Such an ideal cycle helps them maintain their stature. However, not all selective schools share the same success. For example, Historically Black Colleges and Universities (HBCUs) like Howard are selective as they offer education to a specific demographic but struggle to keep their students through to graduation. The lower retention leads to less donation from those who eventually succeed. Incoming students are forced to take out loans which can discourage them from their pursuit of academics (College Guide for HBCU Students | BestColleges, 2023). We are also met with schools with high acceptance rates and high retention. These are niche schools with either religious affiliations or military affiliation. Military schools are noteworthy in this regard boasting a retention of over 90%. This can be attributed to the “free rides” given to all freshmen in return for service to the country after they graduate. This, like elite colleges, alleviates financial burdens from the students and allows them to focus on academics. However, being sponsored by the government leaves them vulnerable to changes in policy. Planned closure or reduced funding can significantly impact retention. However, most schools don’t fall into one of the three categories as they don’t have the financial resources, niche, or don’t cater towards a specific demographic like HBCUs. Many schools value the diversity within their incoming cohorts and actively implement frameworks to facilitate smooth transitions for these student groups within their institutions. For example, we can look at the financial backgrounds of students and the support they receive to assess if some groups are more vulnerable than others. NCES notes that students from low income families are almost three times as likely to graduate at 13% compared to 5% from wealthy families (Status Dropout Rates, 2013). One study notes that healthy relationships between students and professors are the most influential friendships in college as they not only make the students feel at home, but they also lead to budding ambitions and increased interest in academia (Flemming, 2015). This link of support becomes evident in Dawson et al. (2020) where they note a direct relationship between support services available to the student, either internal (professors) or external (organizations which support low income students like Stay the Course) led to a 21% jump in student retention in the first year. Most studies focus on low income students, a lack of literature on the impact of support on middle and high income students implies that it is not as crucial to them. This can be attributed to the high mean scores by these two income groups on college entrance exams which showcase the level of college preparedness (College Board, 2019). However, a study would have been helpful to substantiate these claims. Stewart (2015) alerts colleges that first generation students need relatively more support, both inside and outside class from the onset of their college experience. This can be attributed to the limited exposure of these students to the college environment. For example, a study shows how the lack of guidance by parents on which college preparation classes to take often leads to low grades and even more unfortunate, they are unaware of how to seek and utilize support groups on their campuses (Choy, n.d.) High retention schools have noticed this and have implemented measures to expose vulnerable groups like first generation students to these systems. As the New York Times observes, these colleges collect data from the students before they set foot on campus. When they arrive, “they deepen their connections to other people on campus” from their roommates to their professors to invoke a sense of belonging to the student. (“Opinion | the College Dropout Crisis (Published 2019),” 2023). To compensate for the lack of guidance by parents, these colleges aim to put their students at the center of college resources, each student as a hub from which, ideally, any type of support they need is a call or a walk away. This is significant as there is a direct relationship between an increased sense of belonging and increased student retention (Pedler et al., 2022). This is confirmed by Morales (2014), where schools can create requirements where students have to attend university events like career fairs, have to borrow a book from the library, schedule a meeting with a mentor, and have to ask a TA or RA a question. By exposing students to these connections, they not only know who in the community they can seek help from, but they find new friends (some in high places) in the process. We also find that residential students are more likely to persist through college than commuter students highlighting the importance of reinforcing positive bonds whichever form they may be to the student (Skahill, 2016). We can only take too much. A college student is supposed to be a master of most trades on campus. Vibrant social life, unblemished academic prowess, a working prototype of career progression, peak physical health, and some sort of financial stability. We see that financial woes negatively impact retention. The more work hours a student has, the more likely they are not to graduate (Hartley, 2011). This can be attributed to reduced hours spent on studying or attending tutor sessions which lowers their GPA. In schools that advocate for holistic development, the ramifications extend beyond the classroom. Less time to interact with peers either at the dorms or in extracurricular activities diminishes the sense of belonging and reduces their chances of graduating (Pedler et al., 2022). Falling academics leads to a negative trend on a student’s mental and physical health. A poor mental health standing adversely affects a student’s “concentration, dependability… hindering performance” and a stronger variance of this, depression, increases the chances of dropping out (Consequences of Student Mental Health Issues – Suicide Prevention Resource Center, 2015). This loop isn’t just started by unstable finances. A lower GPA or poor mental health can very well diminish a student’s involvement in campus with the development of imposter syndrome: where a student feels they don’t belong as they don’t match up with their peers. This can set off the same set of dominoes leaving the student in a vicious cycle of trying to achieve the idealized student status. From the individual working extra hours, we can look at groups surrounding the student. A study shows that of all networks formed in college, the one with their friends “substantially” has an impact on their decisions, among them staying in college (Bank et al., 1990). A stressed out student who is underperforming can influence their group to perform poorly and also increase their chances of dropping out especially if they occupy a high position in that group dynamic. This situation was made worse during the pandemic when everyone went remote. One study Son et al. (2020) recorded a 71% jump in the number of students who felt their mental health deteriorated due to increased stress and anxiety, of these students only 5% reached out. Though we can try to pinpoint what increased this level of stress by looking at separation from friends and anxiety on their wellbeing, increased online classes with different time zones affecting sleep schedules, gloomy economic outlook, financial woes due to job layoffs, and the negative environments some households have, none can be quantified to either have the greatest impact on mental health or enough for a case of dropout to be a sum of these factors as it wasn’t the first time students studied remotely. The interplay of these factors and the compounding of these stressors led to an outcome greater than the sum of its parts. The result, however, was quantifiable: a 26% decrease in college persistence from freshmen (Núria Rodríguez‐Planas, 2022). Drawing our gaze back to structure, a study which could have investigated the relationship between the amount of safety nets deployed by different states and the rate of retention would be useful at this juncture. It would be interesting to note if states with emphasis and awareness on the impact of mental health on student performance performed better on retention due to easier access to mental health services. States can pass legislation to pressure schools to come up with diverse mental health centers, mandated school breaks, and other measures to relieve stress directly affecting students. College retention remains a complex issue because of its moving parts. No silver bullet exists but we can try to close the gaps and make more students don the graduation regalia.
