The attacks on 9/11 led the government to increase the leeway that government agencies in the USA can justify surveillance as passed by Congress as the USA Patriot Act (2001) (United States of Surveillance, 2019). Surveillance by itself remains an issue two decades later as people weigh the infringement of the right to privacy against their own security the government says it only wants to protect. Surveillance has come to be negatively connoted with spying with 53% of the American public believing the government is ‘spying’ on them (Shelbourne, 2018). In my conversation with Mr. Chris, a T-Mobile employee, I had the chance to discuss its impact not just in the United States. Mr. Chris is mostly against it as he values the freedom of privacy and the informed consent of people in providing the information. He does this by expressing concern for how far government agencies like the FBI and the CIA harvest data in the name of safety in the daily lives of the innocent. He notes cases of targeted surveillance leading to coercion and suppression in parts of the world. He, however, believes governments are mostly good but he wouldn’t discount scenarios where the government or his own company surveils for its own objectives in a world ever more starved for data. Mr. Patrick's concern for targeted surveillance was one I didn’t see coming. The Economist notes an example of this in China where protesters are tracked as easily as criminals by a system meant to protect the public (The Economist, 2023). This would be defined by Berlin as a violation of negative liberty as the demonstrators do not have freedom to freely protest which is a risk of being detained (Berlin 1969). Weaponized surveillance reduces the ability of people to protest and come up with new ideas. Freedom of expression, an important value in democracies, is taken away instead of nourished by keeping the citizens safe as was promised to them. His concern about targeted surveillance here in the US is real and justified. Police and government agencies continually use biased algorithms in surveillance which routinely misidentify and lead to wrongful conviction of underprivileged groups especially people of color in the United States (Privacy & Racial Justice, 2022). We see an infringement of fairness as the right to equal protection under the law has been violated. The impact of wrongful convictions cannot be understated: loss of income, damage to reputation, and even life behind bars. A wrongful conviction of a person already underprivileged in society makes it worse. This comes at odds with Rawl’s second principle on social justice, difference, which emphasizes that for fairness, policies in place should favor the least-advantaged members of society (Rawls, 1971). Policies in line with the difference principle would ensure that these databases should be utilized, for example, to facilitate community-oriented policing strategies that aim to reduce crime rates in areas predominantly occupied by underrepresented groups which typically are considered less safe as noted by Rodriguez (2021). Lack of safety evidenced by high crime and violence is a sufficient causes of an unequal society. In the paper by Neckerman and Torche (2007), we are notified of causes of inequality such as disparities in income, education, security, and employment opportunities. Limited access to quality education and employment perpetuates cycles of poverty and social exclusion, driving individuals and communities into marginalized states and are sufficient causes as one cannot find the resources to escape the circle of poverty. Additionally, weak law enforcement and judicial systems exacerbate these disparities by failing to address criminal behavior effectively and are necessary causes as they contribute to inequality but aren’t sufficient as the people themselves need the capacity to ascend the social strata. Interactions among these factors form a complex causal structure where lawlessness leads to limited access to education and employment, contributing to higher crime rates as more youth find themselves in the “pipeline” of crime without accepted ways to make ends meet. In turn, elevated crime rates perpetuate disparities by hindering economic development and community safety. This reinforcing feedback loop exacerbates inequality, creating a cycle that is difficult to break. Proper surveillance can play a pivotal role in addressing inequality. Surveillance can be utilized to monitor and identify areas with high crime rates, enabling targeted interventions such as community policing and social programs to address underlying socioeconomic issues. This helps creates an attractive environment for investors who might bring employment opportunities to the impacted communities. This might, in turn, motivate children to complete school and apply for jobs they know are available. Figure 1: A flowchart showing feedback loops, primary causes, and subtle causes of inequality. We see how proper targeted surveillance can reduce inequality through higher order effects caused by creating an environment that attracts investors and encourages students to finish college. I agree with Mr. Chris on the importance of the government creating a secure atmosphere for all its citizens. I value the welfare of the people as a whole and I would place the value of life over all else. A safe country is usually backed up by investors who bring resources critical for development leading to an overall increase in prosperity of the people in the form of the human development index (HDI) which among its many variables, six variables have a strong correlation with the happiness level of a country (Hall & Helliwell, 2014). Equipped with this knowledge, a act utilitarian who believes that choices must be made that would produce good outcomes in the long run Emmons (1973), would argue that the aggregate safety of the citizens would lead to a greater aggregate of happiness than long-term instability caused by an avoidable event that could have been easily prevented had the government had the information beforehand. I would agree with Petit (2016), in arguing against the case of absolute personal freedoms from the state, especially if it’s a choice of security or privacy. Petit would note a case of a libertarian fallacy in surveillance arises when individuals prioritize absolute personal freedom and minimal government intervention over considerations of collective security and societal well-being. I believe this overlooks the potential benefits of surveillance in preventing crime and protecting public safety, leading to a neglect of the broader social implications and the need for a balanced approach to security measures. In the context of surveillance, the decision to prioritize collective security over individual privacy emerges as the most viable approach. Utilitarianism provides a compelling ethical framework, emphasizing the maximization of overall societal welfare. Balancing the interests of the majority and the protection of individual rights is crucial. While respecting privacy is essential, the potential benefits of surveillance in preventing harm and ensuring public safety outweigh individual privacy concerns. A prevailing counterargument, critics may argue that increased surveillance infringes upon individual privacy rights without guaranteeing enhanced security. However, by implementing transparent and accountable surveillance practices and prioritizing targeted interventions over mass surveillance, ethical concerns surrounding privacy can be addressed. Additionally, ensuring robust oversight mechanisms and legal safeguards can mitigate the risks of abuse and protect individual rights. Therefore, we can work towards an approach to surveillance grounded in act utilitarian principles, informed consent of the public, and transparency by various governments to the extent to which their data might be used to regain confidence of the public. This can offer the most just solution to the complex challenges posed by surveillance concerns to ensure human lives are preserved.
