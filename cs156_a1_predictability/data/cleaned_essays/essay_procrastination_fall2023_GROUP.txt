Procrastination, defined as delaying or postponing tasks, is a common yet complex problem among college students (Kim, 2022), manifesting in their approach to assignments. While it's easy to mistake this behavior for laziness or poor time management, procrastination is, in fact, an irrational decision students subconsciously make despite being aware of the potential negative outcomes, such as heightened stress and reduced grades (Kuftyak, 2022).

The underlying reasons for procrastination are complex, as they span a range of psychological and societal factors—from emotional intelligence and utility theory to broader cultural norms. This paper aims to delve into the causes of procrastination among college students during assignment completion. Unraveling these reasons may pave the way for significant changes in educational frameworks, potentially enhancing academic performance, managing stress levels, and fostering overall student well-being.

Academic procrastination can be understood by examining key factors that influence a student’s tendency to procrastinate. To understand this, we can map each student based on the amount of work they have, the time they have to complete it, and their level of conscientiousness (Steel, 2007). For instance, a student with a high workload, ample time, and low conscientiousness is likelier to procrastinate than a student with a similar workload, less time, and high conscientiousness (Dewitte & Schouwenburg, 2002).

Moving beyond individual factors, a multilayered analysis sheds light on the complexities of procrastination. This approach spans from personal psychological influences to the wider academic and societal contexts that shape student behaviors. At the individual level, psychological factors are crucial. For example, a student's fear of failure might lead them to delay starting an assignment.

On the interpersonal level, the influence of peer and social behaviors becomes significant. If procrastination is prevalent within a peer group or seen as a normative response to academic stress, individual students are likely to adopt similar patterns.

At the institutional level, factors such as the intensity of the curriculum, the nature of assignment deadlines, and the support systems available for time management and mental health can significantly influence student behavior. For instance, an academic culture that emphasizes high performance with minimal support for stress management might inadvertently encourage procrastination as students struggle to cope with pressure.

Finally, considering the societal level, broader societal values and beliefs about education and work ethic come into play. Cultural attitudes towards time management, academic achievement, and the perceived value of education can profoundly influence student behavior, including their propensity to procrastinate.

It is within this multifaceted approach that the emotional dimensions of procrastination come to light. The roots of procrastination are grounded in an incomplete understanding of these emotions — be it a fear of falling short of expectations, a genuine disinterest in the task at hand, or an overwhelming sense of being unable to function. Ineffectively interpreting these emotions as signals worsens the issue; instead of seeing them as cues for action or adjustment, individuals may succumb to emotional paralysis, paving the way for procrastination. Managing procrastination entails mastering the ability to navigate the emotional challenges tied to tasks. This involves adopting healthy coping mechanisms, breaking down tasks into more manageable components, and cultivating a resilient mindset capable of overcoming emotional hurdles(Sirois, 2023).

Considering the complex interaction of individual psychology with broader social and cultural influences, Beck’s Cognitive Model provides a fundamental framework, demonstrating how negative cognitions and emotions about academic tasks can trigger a physiological stress response, leading to procrastination (Beck, 1979). For instance, a student’s fear of failure (cognition) can induce anxiety (emotion), resulting in physical symptoms like a racing heart (physiology), which then culminate in avoidance behaviors (procrastination), and reinforce their fear and this loop goes on.

Further, Kahneman and Tversky’s Dual-Process Theory elucidates the conflict between the impulsive, emotion-driven System 1 and the rational, logical System 2 (Kahneman, 2011). In the context of procrastination, System 1 often propels students towards immediate gratification or discomfort avoidance, overpowering the long-term planning and benefits considered by System 2. Additionally, the Self-Determination Theory highlights the lack of intrinsic motivation in tasks that do not align with a student’s interests or values, contributing to procrastination (Deci & Ryan, 1985). The disconnect between extrinsically imposed academic requirements and intrinsic personal goals can create an internal dissonance, leading students to defer tasks.

However, it is essential to acknowledge the limitations of these theories in fully encapsulating the complex nature of procrastination. Individual differences, such as personality traits and life experiences, significantly influence procrastination tendencies (Steel, 2007). Moreover, the generalizability of these theories can vary across different cultural and educational contexts (Klassen et al., 2008).

The influence of procrastination on decision-making ability can be mathematically represented to illustrate how procrastination affects choices:

(C - k t ^ 2) describes the utility function for procrastination. C is the constant initial perceived utility from procrastination. This variable affects the immediate satisfaction a student feels when choosing to procrastinate. k is a coefficient representing the student's subjective attitude toward procrastination. It determines how rapidly the utility of procrastination decreases as the deadline approaches. t is the time interval from assignment release to deadline.

(D + a t ^ 2) is the utility function for not procrastinating. D is the initial utility for starting assignments early. This constant reflects a student's baseline satisfaction or benefit in being proactive about their work. a is a coefficient representing the student's subjective attitude toward completing assignments. A higher a value indicates a more rapid increase in utility over time. Time means the same as in the left part.

The quadratic terms represent the increasing urgency (negative for procrastination, positive for assignment completion) as the deadline approaches. When the utility coming from procrastination is higher than the utility coming from getting stuff done, the rational decision process is affected, and students make irrational decisions (Yan et al., 2022).

Incorporating Prospect Theory, students' procrastination reflects non-rational decision-making. (Jiao, Q, 2011). Using this theory, we see that procrastination is affected by various factors, such as bad choices and possibility effect. For instance, students start tasks late (bad choice) with the hope that they will get them done in time (possibility effect).

The OCEAN criteria introduces us to different types of personality traits people have. Employing the subjective part of our utility model to this formula informs us that the different traits can leave some students more likely to procrastinate.

For students with high conscientiousness, their initial value of C is low due to their natural aversion to delaying tasks, and the effect of the quadratic term - kt^2 is minimal since t, the elapsed time, is small. However, as days pass and t increases, this quadratic term snowballs because of its high k value. This growth signifies a steep increase in procrastination's disutility/negative impact. As a result, highly conscientious students feel the urgency to begin their assignments, finding no utility in procrastination.

Conversely, for those with lower levels of conscientiousness, their low sense of responsibility and poor organizational skills, make them have a higher C(initial comfort) value and a lower k value. As time passes since the assignment's announcement, the negative impact of procrastination grows, but at a more leisurely pace. Disutility increase is less aggressive, allowing these students to remain in the procrastination phase longer.

Consider the utility function for non-procrastination, D + at^2. Highly conscientious students find an intrinsic value in starting assignments early, indicated by a high D. Based on the assumption in our utility function above, as time progresses, their satisfaction in working towards assignment completion (at^2) rises rapidly, propelled by their high a value and reflecting a strong positive attitude toward task completion. The closer the deadline, the more satisfaction they derive from their efforts, motivating them to keep progressing and frequently meeting deadlines (Diener, E., 2019).

In contrast, students with lower levels of conscientiousness experience a milder slope in this utility landscape. Their initial satisfaction in starting tasks early D is lower, and the rate at which their satisfaction grows over time (the a value) is also less steep. Therefore, as time elapses since the assignment release, the increase in their utility or satisfaction from working on the assignment does not accelerate as sharply.

The ADDRESSING model alerts us that factors such as gender and economic background can affect personalities as well. In college, it has been found that on average, male students tend to procrastinate more than female students as they exhibit behavior that promotes a ”task-averting situation” (Khan et al, 2014). This, adding to the OCEAN criteria makes us aware of the influence of personality traits on a student’s ability to keep deadlines.

Understanding why college students procrastinate involves looking at a mix of emotional, social, and cultural factors. Whether it's the emotional hurdles tied to tasks, the societal influence on behavior, or the different types of procrastination like stress-induced delays or impulsive behaviors, it's a complex picture. Ultimately, dealing with procrastination is an ongoing journey that requires practical approaches, considering not just academic aspects but also the well-being of students in the broader context of education.
