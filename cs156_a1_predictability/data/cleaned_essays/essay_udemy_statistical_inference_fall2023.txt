The increased usage of e-learning has brought new opportunities for students and instructors alike. Sites like Udemy, for example, allow instructors to teach a course of their liking. Some instructors are more successful than others and this paper aims to help new instructors better position their channels for success. A common benchmark of success is the number of subscribers a channel has. This report investigates if there is indeed a difference in the number of subscribers within a difficulty range. To do this, we will use a coalesced sample dataset from (Larxel, 2020), the first 10 rows can be outputted from Appendix A. Our sample contains 300 channels, of which 103 are beginner-level courses. We will investigate these two variables: our dependent variable is the quantitatively discrete number of subscribers (quantitatively discrete as these are integers from which we can carry out operations on them e.g calculate the mean to produce further insight into our data) and our independent variable as the qualitatively ordinal difficulty level (qualitatively ordinal as the difficulty level is categorically ordered. They are also irregular as we can’t assure that the difference between the beginner and intermediate levels is the same as the difficulty between the intermediate and expert levels. Moreover, the fact that the instructors choose the difficulty level means they’re subjective). Here, we assume that every student who subscribed “attended” the lessons. A statistic difference can serve as advice on how the population of Udemy instructors can structure their courses’ difficulty to increase success.

Firstly, we use descriptive statistics on the two groups. We see the mean subscriber count of beginner level courses is 2238 while for non-beginner level is 3087. We find the standard deviation to be interesting as it is greater than the means showing great variability, that our data is not a cluster, in our dataset. This can be attributed to the outliers, the super successful instructors, as can be seen in that the modes are instructors with no subscribers. This inflates the range as it is now only the instructor with the largest number of subscribers. This can be seen in Appendix A. This can also complicate our analysis of standard errors and leads us to create a histogram of the number of subscribers to give a better visual of the lack of clustering suggested by the measures of spread.

The sky-blue histogram on the left shows the frequency of beginner-level subscribers while the coral histogram on the right shows the frequency of non-beginner-level subscribers. Both are skewed heavily to the right, this can be attributed to a means bigger than the median and modes. The modes are the first bin in each case as seen by the peak. The histogram at first glance shows more similarities than differences with the shape of the frequency distribution. This can be attributed to the very successful instructors in each case. However, the mean and median subscriber count are both higher for non-beginner levels. At this juncture, it is useful to invoke significance tests.

Randomness: We can assume this for our sample due to the number of courses available. Normal distribution: This is not satisfied as the data is heavily right-skewed. The central limit theorem assures us that the ensuing distribution of the sample mean is well approximated by a normal distribution. We know that our samplings will follow the law of regression to the mean and the peak of our distribution is more likely to reflect the population mean. However, since we cannot assure randomness, there might be an inherent bias in our calculation distorting the estimates of the parameters if, for example, successful beginners are unrepresented, we might falsely reject the null. Moreover, we ignore possible confounding variables like pricing and real-world demand which might affect success regardless of difficulty. We also don’t need to apply the Finite Population Correction as our sample size is under the 5% threshold of the total population.

We first need to calculate the standard error (SE) for the difference between the two means from which we can create our confidence intervals to get a feeling of the overlap between the two distributions. We can create the interval as our sample has qualified for review under the CLT. We find the standard error as 765.58 in Appendix C. To quantify this, we can calculate Cohen’s d when the sample sizes are different. We get a pooled standard deviation of 13242.9 and a Cohen’s d of 0.064 indicating a very small effect size hence a visibly small difference in the two means that cannot be seen by the naked eye. This indicates very low practical significance. This can be seen in Appendix E. To calculate our confidence interval, 5 we get a point estimate of 849.02 from the difference of the two means. We then calculate the degrees of freedom from our sample with unequal sample t-distribution due to lack of info on the parameters. I then calculated the critical t for the distribution to calculate the p-value and power. sizes to get: 295.489, which follows from our large sample size and will give us better confidence of rejecting the null.

It is also worthwhile to note that the range of our interval is quite high (3000) showing reduced precision from high variability. From our critical-t, we can compare it to our p-value to see if the observations beyond critical t are sufficient for us to reject the null. We find the t-value to be 1.09 which is closer to our mean-point 0 than our critical-t. This registers a p-value of 0.268 as can be seen in Appendix F. This means our observations align more with the null than the alternative as our p-value is greater than our alpha value. Consequently, our null hypothesis is true: beginner-level courses don’t have more subscribers than non-beginner-level courses. Our online instructors at Udemy, given our type 1 error rate which is equal to alpha, are at liberty to choose any course difficulty without worrying about the impact on its subscribers. This argument is reliable because if there was a noticeable preference on the difficulty levels, most instructors wouldn’t bother with the other as they all want to be successful.

However, it is critical to check if we upheld the null wrongly: the chance of not rejecting the null hypothesis given the alternative is true (a type 2 error).

We get this a 5% chance and our power function as 94.65% since it is the complement of the type 2 error. A type 1 error would, therefore, mean that we mislead the instructors by saying difficulty doesn’t matter while a type 2 error would mean we mislead the instructors by saying difficulty does matter. A type 2 error could be worse as Udemy could find itself saturated with only beginner-level material leading to a “bad” reputation with experts.
