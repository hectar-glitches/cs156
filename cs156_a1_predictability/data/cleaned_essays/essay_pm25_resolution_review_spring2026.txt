Surface PM2.5 is a central air-quality metric because it is closely tied to population exposure and health risk in urban environments. Chemical transport models (CTMs) are widely used to forecast PM2.5 and evaluate mitigation strategies, and it is often assumed that increasing spatial or temporal resolution should improve performance by resolving sharper gradients and faster variability. However, multi-resolution studies frequently find diminishing returns: modeled surface PM2.5 changes less with finer horizontal grids than with changes in boundary-layer representation, suggesting that vertical mixing is often the limiting factor rather than resolution alone. This literature review asks: When does increasing spatial or temporal resolution improve modeled surface PM2.5 , and when do differences in boundary-layer mixing limit that improvement? The review argues that resolution benefits are conditional on meteorological regime: higher resolution tends to help when horizontal structure dominates (e.g., strong gradients or complex terrain/coastal flows), but improvements are constrained when errors arise primarily from boundary-layer depth and mixing timing. Evidence is synthesized from studies that (i) compare multiple resolutions and boundary-layer schemes in CTMs, (ii) evaluate against surface monitoring networks while accounting for representativeness, and (iii) use complementary remote-sensing constraints to assess spatial aerosol patterns. State of the Science (SOTS) PM2.5 model skill is commonly assessed by comparing modeled near-surface mass concentrations to monitoring networks using bias and correlation statistics, interpreted against established PM evaluation benchmarks. (Simon et al., 2012) A central complication is that Eulerian CTMs predict grid-cell mean concentrations whereas monitors are point measurements that can reflect local siting and neighborhood-scale heterogeneity. (Park et al., 2006) This scale mismatch creates representativeness error, and it can become more consequential as grid spacing decreases because finer grids reduce spatial averaging and make comparisons more sensitive to unresolved near-field gradients and to inconsistencies between the resolution of emissions inputs and the resolution of the simulation. (Henne et al., 2010) Accordingly, neighborhood-scale evaluations emphasize whether improvements reflect physically plausible changes in transport and mixing rather than simply aligning model structure with monitor locations. (U.S. Within this evaluation framework, the resolution literature is often summarized by a regime-dependent pattern: horizontal refinement can improve PM2.5 when the dominant error is the spatial placement or numerical smoothing of pollution features, but the marginal benefit can diminish when surface concentrations are controlled primarily by vertical dilution in the planetary boundary layer. (U.S. Because most PM2.5 emissions enter the atmosphere at or near the surface, biases in the depth and timing of boundary-layer mixing translate directly into biases in near-surface concentration even if horizontal transport is represented well. (Park et al., 2006) For that reason, many resolution studies interpret PM2.5 errors alongside meteorological variables that note dilution and removal conditions e.g boundary-layer height, winds, precipitation, and humidity, to distinguish horizontal misplacement from mixing- or deposition-driven bias. (U.S. Satellite aerosol optical depth (AOD) is frequently used as a complementary constraint because it can evaluate whether the model captures the regional spatial pattern of aerosol burden on cloud-free days that sparse monitor networks cannot fully sample. (van Donkelaar et al., 2010) AOD is obtained by inverting top-of-atmosphere radiance under Beer–Lambert-type attenuation assumptions, so retrievals depend on assumed aerosol optics and surface reflectance as well as cloud screening. (Chu et al., 2016) Because AOD is column-integrated extinction rather than near-surface mass, agreement with AOD can indicate improved regional placement while surface PM2.5 remains biased if vertical mixing allocates aerosol to the wrong heights. (Liu et al., 2005) This is why many studies combine surface monitors (for exposure-relevant near-surface mass) with AOD (for spatial context) and, where available, vertical profile information (e.g., lidar) to determine whether remaining errors are fundamentally vertical. (Winker et al., 2010) When higher resolution helps Clear benefits from finer horizontal or temporal resolution are most often reported when the main limitation is that coarse grids blur transport-driven structure and smooth away localized enhancements that matter for exposure. Neighborhood-scale modeling frameworks emphasize that refinement is most defensible when the scientific or policy question is inherently local and the emissions and meteorology supplied to the model are detailed enough to support the refined grid. (U.S.

A physically transparent example is the sea-/lake-breeze circulation. Differential heating between land and water generates a pressure gradient that drives onshore flow, and the associated convergence can concentrate pollutants into narrow shoreline bands; in this regime, refinement helps primarily by better resolving where convergence and recirculation occur. (Jacob, 1999) Exposure-focused applications similarly show that finer grids can make local source corridors (e.g., transportation and industrial features) appear more distinctly in modeled PM2.5 patterns, which is often muted at coarser resolution. (Isakov et al., 2007) At the same time, the literature stresses that resolution gains are only meaningful when inputs are consistent with the refined scale. If a coarse emissions inventory is downscaled with limited activity information, fine-grid structure may look realistic without being well constrained, so studies commonly pair resolution experiments with emissions allocation checks and meteorological evaluation. (Zheng et al., 2017) Evaluation scale can also confound interpretation: because monitors are point measurements and CTMs represent grid-cell means, finer grids can increase representativeness error and inflate apparent scatter in model–monitor comparisons even when the underlying flow representation is improved. (Henne et al., 2010) Taken together, these results motivate a conditional view of refinement: it is most valuable when the controlling limitation is horizontal placement of mesoscale circulations and local gradients, and least valuable when the dominant limitation is vertical mixing or removal. (U.S.

For example, under anticyclonic high pressure, large-scale subsidence warms air on top adiabatically and strengthens a capping in-version that limits mixed-layer growth and promotes stagnation; in such episodes, refining the horizontal grid can sharpen maps while leaving the dominant dilution error unchanged if the model misrepresents stability or subsidence strength. (Jacob, 1999)

Small timing errors in the morning transition or evening collapse can therefore generate large PM2.5 errors without any change in horizontal resolution. (Hong et al., 2006) Because turbulent vertical dilution occurs at scales smaller than the model grid, it is represented with parameterizations rather than being explicitly resolved, so sensitivities depend strongly on the boundary-layer scheme. Different PBL closures represent turbulence in different ways, e.g, a local TKE (turbulent kinetic energy) scheme computes mixing from local stability (so mixing weakens strongly in stable nocturnal conditions), whereas a nonlocal K-profile scheme is tuned to represent convective mixed layers where large eddies create transport that isn’t well described by local gradients. (Hong et al., 2006) Persistent biases are often interpreted by testing whether model errors covary with dilution indicators such as PBL height and near-surface winds rather than with location alone, which is consistent with mixing-limited behavior. (U.S. Removal-limited regimes provide a second pathway where refinement alone may not help. During precipitation or very humid periods, wet deposition and aerosol water uptake can dominate near-surface PM2.5 evolution, so the controlling uncertainty can be removal and phase partitioning rather than the horizontal placement of emissions. (Boylan and Russell, 2006) In such cases, finer grids may sharpen spatial patterns while leaving episode-mean bias and error largely unchanged if loss processes remain misrepresented. (Boylan and Russell, 2006) This distinction also motivates why assimilation can matter more than refinement in mixing-limited regimes. Resolution refinement is a bottom-up change to the forward CTM simulation (sharper resolved transport), whereas data assimilation is a top-down constraint that uses observations to update the evolving state when key controls on dilution are biased. (Evensen, 2003) For retrospective reanalysis, smoothing over a fixed time window can use later observations to constrain earlier states, whereas operational forecasting requires sequential filtering because future observations are unavailable. (Carrassi et al., 2017) Because boundary-layer mixing remains parameterized, many CTMs rely on K-theory closures which relate turbulent fluxes to local gradients; this approximation can miss nonlocal transport by large eddies at the mixed-layer top. (Hong et al., 2006) In mixing-limited episodes, assimilating constraints on boundary-layer structure (e.g., mixing height proxies) and column aerosol information (AOD) can therefore correct correlated state errors that persist across horizontal resolutions. (Carrassi et al., 2017)

For resolution-sensitivity questions, AOD is most informative as an independent check on whether higher-resolution simulations improve the spatial placement of aerosol features on clear-sky days, including the coastal and transport-organized structures discussed above. (van Donkelaar et al., 2010) However, AOD does not uniquely determine surface PM2.5 because it integrates extinction through the full column rather than sampling near-surface mass. (Liu et al., 2005) This limitation connects directly to mixing-limited regimes: a model can match AOD while still biasing surface PM2.5 if aerosol is vertically allocated incorrectly, for example by mixing too shallowly (concentrating mass near the surface) or placing too much aerosol aloft (reducing surface mass for a given column burden). (Liu et al., 2005) A practical complication is sampling. Passive AOD retrievals require sufficiently cloud-free pixels, so AOD is often missing during the same unsettled or overcast regimes when boundary-layer depth and removal processes vary rapidly and PM2.5 sensitivity to mixing is high. (Levy et al., 2013) Where available, vertical profile information from active remote sensing (e.g., CALIPSO lidar) helps reduce this ambiguity by distinguishing boundary-layer aerosol from elevated layers, which is directly relevant for diagnosing whether resolution changes are limited by vertical mixing rather than horizontal placement. (Winker et al., 2010) Together, these points imply that remote sensing contributes most directly to this review when it is used to evaluate spatial aerosol patterns across resolutions on clear days and to test whether remaining surface bias is consistent with vertical allocation and mixing errors. (Winker et al., 2010) Uncertainties and future directions A central reason studies disagree on whether “higher resolution helps” is that resolution experiments differ in what they hold fixed, so the apparent benefit of refinement depends on whether the dominant error source is horizontal placement, vertical mixing, emissions allocation, or removal. (U.S. Even evaluation itself can shift conclusions: because monitors are point measurements while CTMs represent grid-cell means, representativeness error can penalize higher-resolution simulations by exposing unresolved near-field variability. (Henne et al., 2010) Therefore, refinement should be interpreted conditionally: it is most likely to add value when it corrects demonstrably unresolved spatial placement and timing errors, and least likely to help when biases persist across resolutions and covary with mixing and removal conditions. (Boylan and Russell, 2006) Remote sensing introduces additional uncertainty because AOD retrievals are inverse products and because cloud screening creates systematic missingness during unsettled meteorological regimes. (Chu et al., 2016) This means that “no AOD” is not neutral information; it often coincides with periods when boundary-layer evolution and wet removal are rapidly changing and when mixing-limited surface PM2.5 errors are plausible. (Levy et al., 2013) Profile observations can reduce ambiguity by constraining whether aerosol resides within the boundary layer or in elevated layers that affect AOD but not surface mass. (Winker et al., 2010) Because refinement is a forward-model (bottom-up) change while assimilation is an observation-constrained (top-down) correction, mixing-limited regimes are natural candidates for ensemble Kalman filtering that uses mixing-height or aerosol-profile information to reduce correlated state errors. (Evensen, 2003) Future work should therefore diagnose regimes explicitly by (i) comparing CTM errors against surface monitors and AOD to identify misplaced horizontal structure, (van Donkelaar et al., 2010) (ii) testing emissions and temporal-profile consistency at the refined scale, (Zheng et al., 2017) and (iii) adding lidar mixing-height constraints to evaluate or assimilate PBL depth in regional CTMs, with success judged by reduced PM2.5 bias during stagnant or removal-limited episodes. (Pleim, 2007) A conditional strategy calls out: use finer grids when model error comes from putting pollution features in the wrong horizontal location, but focus on boundary-layer evaluation and improvement when the main error comes from how strongly the model removes pollutants vertically.
