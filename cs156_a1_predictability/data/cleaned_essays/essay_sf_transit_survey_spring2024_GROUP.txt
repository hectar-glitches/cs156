Our chosen Supporting Action is TLU.7-1, which states to ‘launch a public awareness campaign, including messaging tailored to specific communities, with the goal of educating residents about the health, economic, and environmental benefits of transit, active transportation, and electric vehicles’ (San Francisco Environment Department, 2021).

Before brainstorming a concrete solution, we must identify the constraints of implementing the supporting action. There are two major constraints. Firstly, funding for the campaign is limited, a maximum of 10 million is given to reach the goal of 400000 mtCO2e deduction (San Francisco Environment Department), which necessitates cost-effective strategies. This is nonnegotiable as it is part of a bill issued by the federal government. Secondly, the campaign aims to have an urgent impact before 2030, meaning that actions need to be scalable and quickly implementable.

The SFTR’s Transit Justice Coalition is a noteworthy initiative that has been instrumental in addressing equity in public transportation. The coalition is a collaboration among various organizations and community groups in San Francisco that aims to create a public transit system that is equitable and accessible to all residents, especially those who have historically been excluded from transportation planning and decisions. To reach this goal, they pushed to build public transit infrastructure in underserved communities. Their actions have led to enhanced public transit access and usage, which contribute to reduced car dependency and pollution (San Francisco Transit Riders, 2023).

The San Francisco Commuter Benefits Ordinance represents another pivotal campaign. It mandates employers to offer programs that encourage sustainable commuting practices among their employees. This ordinance aims to reduce traffic congestion and greenhouse gas emissions by promoting alternative commuting options. Employers with 20 or more staff are required to comply by offering one of several specified benefits, such as pre-tax deductions for transit costs, employer-paid transit subsidies, or company-provided transportation. While having a seemingly effective strategy, were unable to find a report of any measurable outcomes, and therefore are unable to determine if they were successful (American Legal Publishing, 2024).

While the two existing campaigns have laid a foundation for sustainable urban mobility and environmental awareness, they primarily target underserved community members. This does not directly address the specific behaviors of the larger population, and therefore, does not tackle large-scale effects that may cause people to opt in to non-environmentally friendly services such as Uber. We will adopt a new solution that addresses the broader population.

In California, Uber cabs emit an estimated 494,000 metric tons of CO2 annually. Notably, 30% of these emissions occurred with no passengers in the vehicle (San Francisco County Transportation Authority, 2023). This data underscores the impact of ride-hailing on the city's greenhouse gas emissions and highlights the necessity of educating Uber users about more sustainable transportation alternatives like Muni Buses.

Given the constraints and the gap identified, we came up with a concrete solution to create an educational campaign that targets users of ride-hailing services (Uber, Lift) and informs them of the ecological benefits of using Muni Buses. This would take place either online or in physical locations. To focus on an effective campaign strategy, we designed a survey to understand what effect other campaigns had on our target population. We believe that the survey can provide valuable insights for future action. Survey Analysis To examine the most effective campaign strategies, we conducted a survey. We asked both qualitative and quantitative questions that examined which campaign strategies left the strongest impression and asked additional questions about living location and age. By cross-referencing the data from different questions, we were able to determine which campaigns should be implemented in different districts.

We used systematic sampling. After a consensus, went near bus stops and public parks and sampled each 5th individual as these spots have a higher foot traffic. In the case we were not successful, we shifted to convenience sampling till we got an individual who was willing to answer our survey after which we defaulted to systematic. We didn’t enter buses or parks which would have led to pseudoreplication as we could have been sampling the same families. Our survey had 37 participants, only 32 of whom lived in San Francisco. Based on the report by the city on possible supporting actions to create a more sustainable city, my group looked further into Supporting Action is TLU.7-1. This involved the launch of a public awareness campaign, including messaging tailored to specific communities, with the goal of educating residents about the health, economic, and environmental benefits of transit, active transportation, and electric vehicles. We saw this action as pertinent as the choice of transportation for the daily commute greatly impacts the city’s sustainability metrics.

To propose an actionable campaign plan for the supporting action, we held a survey from which we asked residents of San Francisco about their preferred types and perceived effectiveness of various advertisement methods. The result based on five districts is that social media is the most effective form of advertisement overall. Additionally, residents find that humorous and emotion-evoking advertisements which are played frequently give them the most incentive to act. In terms of the 5 districts with sufficient amounts of data, in Castro, Mission, and Nob Hill 40% or more of the surveyed population were more receptive to social media ads while there was no preference in Chinatown and Pacific Heights.

Past advertising campaigns by San Francisco Transit Riders' (SFTR) Transit Justice Coalition (TJC), and the city’s Commuter Benefits Ordinance can serve in our proposal. Though SFTR only focused on underserved communities and the TJC on motivating employers to avail public transit or active forms of transportation, they were effective in getting these niche of people to use public transport.

Based on this information, though limited especially considering our small sample size, we can employ a means-ends analysis as the stakes are high given how important these goals are in terms of the sustainability of the city. A means-end would involve characterizing the current state and final state and creating a subgoal that attempts to bridge the gap between the current and final state. Though this won’t guarantee the best solution given the size, we are pressed by time and the scaled-down subgoal would reduce costs as the City Government has limited funds for this project. This also gives the city an advantage in monitoring increased awareness in just the proposed districts given the costs associated with the campaign. This adds flexibility to the city government and accountability from the advertising parties. The current state is described by the TLU 7.1 which alerts of an unawareness of the public of the various benefits of public transit. The final state is also implied on TLU 7.1 which is having the public aware of the health, economic, and environmental benefits of public transit after the campaign. The subgoal is a narrowed-down version of TLU .7-1: we decided to focus on creating an educational campaign that targets users of ride-hailing services (Uber, Lift) and informs them of the ecological benefits of using Muni Buses.

It would be prudent to employ and widen the services of SFTR to offer advertisements to more than just underserved communities while using TJC to offer the persuasive component of the advertisement as this resonates most with San Franciscans.
