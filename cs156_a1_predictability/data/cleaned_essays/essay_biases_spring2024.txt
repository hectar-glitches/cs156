LLMs like ChatGPT reflect our cognitive strengths and weaknesses. An example of this is the fact that ChatGPT trained in assessing resumes penalized female applicants as it’s mostly fed on data depicting women as “passive objects” (Stolkel-Walker, 2023). This is an instance of confirmation bias as described by Von Bergen et al. (2018) whereby if we find data that disagrees with our opinion, we hold it “to a much higher standard”. In the case of ChatGPT, the bias emerges in the interpreting phase where the model assigns significance to observed patterns favoring men and recommends them to the hiring committee. When met with a female applicant, however, the model discounts the same achievements and rejects applicants as it has a belief they’re “passive objects” in line with beliefs embedded in the data. An example of an integral emotional bias as noted by Greene and Haidt (2002) might arise when a user who is anxious when they have to make a high-stake submission would take more convincing to accept a response even if it is well thought out. This is because anxiety decreases our likelihood of agreeing when presented with new information as it makes us more risk-averse (Wray and Stone, 2005). My passage highlights specific instances of bias within an LLM, focusing on confirmation bias and an integral emotional bias. I provide an example of gender bias and I illustrate how a user's anxiety can influence their acceptance of the model's responses. The second passage takes a broader approach and mentions confirmation bias, stereotyping, and algorithmic bias as general manifestations of biases in language models. It, however, not only focused on the bias that might affect the user but also provided caution to the users. To mitigate confirmation bias on the part of the LLM, we can employ the blinding method. This, as described by Kassin et al (2013) would involve reducing the amount of contextual information on the resumes to be evaluated by the model. In our case, the company can blind the model by removing gender information so that no one is praised or penalized based on their gender and only qualifications take center stage. For the anxious user heading for a presentation, they can consider employing a break before they make a conclusion on the response from the LLM. The importance of this time delay is that it allows our unsettled psychological state, anxiety in this case, to fall back to baseline states from which we can make better-rationalized decisions as these peak emotions are fleeting (Wilson and Gilbert, 2005). This delay would enable the user to properly fact-check the response to ascertain its relevance which might prove useful when the effect of the bias is minimized.
