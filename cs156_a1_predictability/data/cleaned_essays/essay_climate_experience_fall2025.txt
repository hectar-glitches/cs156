Public understanding of climate change remains deeply polarized in the United States (Funk & Kennedy, 2016). While scientific consensus is overwhelming, belief that climate change is “happening” varies sharply across regions and partisan lines. Prior research has found that personal experience of extreme weather can increase climate concern (Myers et al., 2013). This study therefore examines whether objective exposure to federally declared climate-related disasters predicts belief in climate change, and whether this relationship varies according to partisan context. In particular, it explores whether repeated exposure to natural disasters increases belief in climate change among Republican-leaning counties, potentially narrowing ideological divides on climate change, or whether political identity continues to override experiential evidence. I assembled four county-level datasets. Beliefs come from the Yale Climate Opinion Maps, which provide model-based estimates at the county level validated against national benchmarks (Howe et al., 2019). Objective exposure comes from FEMA disaster declarations since 2000, restricted to climate-relevant incidents (Disaster Declarations Summaries - V2, 2025). Partisan context is proxied by Democratic presidential vote share from the MIT Election Data Lab, aggregated to a stable county-level lean (MIT Election Data and Science Lab, 2018). Median household income and high-school graduation rates from the American Community Survey serve as socioeconomic controls (Walker & Herman, 2025). Figure 1. The county‐level choropleth shows belief is highest along the Pacific, Northeast, and many metro corridors, but it weakens across the interior South and the Mountain West. Many of those interior belts are exactly where FEMA records frequent disaster declarations, especially for wildfire, drought, and flood. If experience alone were doing the work, these places should tilt upward in belief. Instead, a noticeable cluster of sparsely populated, high-disaster counties reports comparatively low belief. This tension motivates the next stage of the study. Why do many low-density counties with frequent disasters resist the “experience increases belief” pattern?. I consider three live explanations that fit prior work. Per-capita severity: exposure counts are high in rural areas but losses per person are small, so residents experience declarations as paperwork rather than threat (Egan & Mullin, 2012). Information and identity: where partisan cues and media echo stronger skepticism, people reinterpret the same events in non-climate terms (Kahan et al., 2012; Hamilton & Safford, 2021). Hazard mix: drought and wildfire may connect less readily to climate beliefs than hurricanes and coastal flooding; if rural places mainly see the former, belief is less responsive (Konisky et al., 2016). A fourth possibility is measurement: county-level belief estimates carry uncertainty that is larger in small populations (Howe et al., 2019). If the severity story is correct, within the same county belief should rise after more intense events (claims per capita, homes burned, insured losses), even if simple declaration counts show little change. If information and identity are the binding constraint, then even intense events will move belief less where Republican vote share is high, local media markets lean conservative, or broadband/news consumption is low. If hazard mix matters, effects will be larger for storm and flood exposure than for drought or wildfire, holding intensity constant. If measurement drives the puzzle, effects should strengthen when we account for estimate precision and when we pool across neighboring rural counties. I will build an annual county panel that extends our current dataset. To measure exposure more credibly than raw declaration counts, I will replace or supplement counts with intensity metrics. Prior studies show that using event severity and proximity improves signal when testing experience-based opinion change (Konisky, Hughes, & Kaylor, 2016). Estimation will follow a difference-in-differences design with county fixed effects (to absorb all stable local traits) and year fixed effects (to absorb national shocks). Following Egan and Mullin (2012), I will add event-study plots from three years before to three years after major events to verify flat pre-trends and visualize post-event change. To test our core moderation claim and the sparse-county anomaly, exposure will be interacted with (a) Republican vote share and (b) population density. Hamilton and Safford (2021) motivate attention to rural identity and density when explaining climate beliefs. Because YCOM’s county estimates carry uncertainty, I will use precision-weight regressions by their published standard errors or fit a partial-pooling hierarchical model that stabilizes estimates for small counties (Howe et al., 2015). If disasters causally raise belief, we should see within-county increases after exposure and larger gains where baseline skepticism is higher (significant exposure × GOP interaction). If effects are merely salience or confounding, pre-trends will slope, event-study leads will be non-zero, or results will vanish when using non-climate placebos (e.g., earthquakes) as in Konisky et al. (2016). Hazard-specific specifications (floods vs. wildfires vs. drought) and intensity doses help rule out simple “any disaster” stories and show where experience is most persuasive (Egan & Mullin, 2012). Our method will allow us to see support for per-capita severity: coefficients on intensity measures are positive and significant in rural counties even when declaration counts are not; the sparse-county gap narrows when exposure is scaled by people or housing. Secondly, support for information and identity: the exposure effect is near zero in low-density, high-Republican counties even at high intensity, and grows with more urban media environments; simple slopes by partisanship diverge after events. Also support for hazard mix: strong post-event belief gains for storms and floods but muted gains for drought and wildfire. Each alternative maps to a falsifiable prediction, and the design checks for spurious findings. Flat pre-trends and null results for placebo hazards (for example, earthquakes) will guard against generic crisis effects. Re-estimating with population weights and with spatially robust standard errors will check that statewide swings or spatial spillovers are not driving results. If per-capita severity and hazard mix explain most of the rural exception, the “experience increases belief” framework still holds but requires better exposure measurement.
