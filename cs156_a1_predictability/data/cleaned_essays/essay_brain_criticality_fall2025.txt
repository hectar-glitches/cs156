The developing brain must balance flexibility with stability as structural connectivity increases. As Olaf Sporns and others have argued, this often places neural systems near an “edge of chaos,” where activity is neither completely disordered nor rigidly synchronized, and small changes in coupling can have large effects on collective dynamics. Statistical physics offers a natural language for this regime. In many interacting systems, increasing coupling drives a transition toward a critical point, beyond which the system locks into highly ordered, synchronized states (Stanley, 1971). Operating near criticality can enhance information transmission and dynamic range (Shew & Plenz, 2013), but overshooting it risks runaway synchronization, which manifests as a seizure-like activity, which is more common during periods of rapid structural maturation (da Silva, 2003). Recent work has shown that these ideas transfer directly to brain networks. Das et al. (2014) demonstrated that a classical Ising model on structural connectivity can reproduce empirical functional coupling patterns, and Craig et al. (2025) extended this with heterogeneous, personalized coupling matrices to capture individual variability. These studies suggest that coupling, criticality, and collective dynamics provide a useful lens on brain organization. Here we apply this framework to development. Using an Ising-model-based Monte Carlo simulation, we let coupling density increase over developmental time on simple network models and track magnetization, susceptibility, and entropy. This allows us to ask when a maturing network approaches criticality, how close it comes to regimes of pathological synchronization, and how different developmental trajectories of structural coupling alter these outcomes. Modeling the brain To investigate how developmental changes in structural connectivity influence large-scale neural dynamics, we model the brain as a network of interacting regions whose activity evolves according to the Ising framework.

We compare two network architectures that differ in their topological organization: a random Erdős–Rényi graph and a small-world Watts–Strogatz graph. Random networks usually exhibit uniformly distributed, unstructured connectivity and serve as a useful baseline for immature, weakly organized brain networks. Small-world networks, in contrast, exhibit high clustering with short average path lengths, reflecting the modular yet integrated structure observed in empirical brain networks. Comparing these architectures allows us to assess how topological features influence the system’s approach to criticality as coupling increases over development.

This rule ensures that the sequence of spin configurations samples from the Boltzmann distribution, allowing the system to explore both low- and high-energy states with their probability comparable to that of their thermodynamic likelihood. As a result, the Metropolis–Hastings update introduces intrinsic stochasticity into the system, capturing spontaneous fluctuations in neural activity. To quantify how the system behaves under each developmental trajectory and network architecture, we measure several standard observables from statistical physics.

Together, these quantities allow us to characterize whether and when the system approaches a critical regime, and how this process differs across developmental profiles and network architectures. Several modeling assumptions are necessary to keep the system tractable while still capturing the mechanisms of interest. First, each brain region is represented as a binary variable, an abstraction that omits the continuous firing rates and complex nature of real neurons but retains the essential interactions required to study collective synchronization. This simplification is standard in large-scale Ising-based brain models and has been shown to reproduce empirical functional connectivity patterns despite its coarse resolution (Craig et al., 2025). Second, temperature is modeled as a single global parameter rather than a region-specific or dynamic quantity. Although real neural systems exhibit heterogeneous and context-dependent noise levels, a uniform temperature provides a controlled way to separate the effects of stochastic fluctuations from those of structural coupling. Third, developmental change is implemented solely through increases in coupling strength rather than through rewiring or changes in excitation–inhibition balance.

These assumptions do not aim to capture the full biological complexity of brain development; rather, they define a simplified but interpretable model in which the contributions of topology, coupling, and stochasticity can be examined in isolation. By focusing on the minimal ingredients necessary for critical phenomena to arise, the model allows us to probe how developmental increases in structural coupling interact with network structure to shape large-scale neural dynamics. Overall, this modeling framework provides a controlled setting in which to examine how different structural organizations and developmental coupling trajectories influence the emergence of coordinated neural states.

Monte Carlo simulation and empirical observables To connect the theoretical predictions to observed behavior, we simulate the stochastic evolution of the Ising system using Monte Carlo methods.

The primary update rule is the single-spin Metropolis–Hastings algorithm, which implements the probabilistic dynamics described in the modeling section.

A sequence of such steps defines a Markov chain on spin configurations whose stationary distribution is the Boltzmann distribution at temperature T . We also implement the Wolff cluster algorithm. The Wolff method updates clusters of aligned spins rather than single sites, greatly reducing critical slowing down. These empirical results support the theoretical picture from our theoretical section. The largest eigenvalue of the structural network predicts the location of the critical coupling in a mean-field sense; heat capacity, susceptibility, and entropy all display characteristic signatures of a continuous transition; Metropolis and cluster dynamics agree on the macroscopic behavior; and the shape of the developmental coupling trajectory modulates the timing, but not the coupling level, at which critical-like dynamics emerge.

Entropy behaves in a complementary manner, remaining high and relatively flat in the disordered regime and then declining as the network enters an ordered phase. These results confirm that, in this simplified model, developmental strengthening of coupling naturally drives the system through a phase-transition-like point where responsiveness and fluctuations are maximal. Comparing architectures, the WS network consistently reaches criticality at a lower coupling density than the ER network, reflecting its larger spectral radius and more structured topology. In contrast, the ER network requires stronger coupling to achieve comparable levels of global synchronization and shows a somewhat broader, less pronounced critical region. From a developmental perspective, this suggests that the emergence of small-world organization can be viewed as a structural “shortcut” to criticality: it allows the maturing brain to enter a regime of high responsiveness and efficient integration without uniformly increasing coupling everywhere. Framed as advice to a hypothetical “developmental engineer” or clinician, the simulations support a strategy of guiding networks toward, but not far beyond, the critical coupling density. In the model, WS-like architectures reach this window earlier and with less total coupling than ER-like random networks, suggesting that developmental processes that promote clustered, short-path connectivity (rather than purely random addition of links) may support efficient transitions into a critical, high-plasticity regime while limiting the need for globally strong coupling.
