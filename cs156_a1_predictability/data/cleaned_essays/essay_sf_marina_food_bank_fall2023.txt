The SF-Marina Food Bank aims to provide food security to residents in the Bay Area, especially the San Francisco and Marina districts. This can’t be understated as 1 out of 4 suffer from hunger due to a high cost of living (Department of Public Health - Food Security Task Force, 2023). The Food Bank aims to reduce this number. Their ultimate goal is to end hunger in San Francisco and Marin (San Francisco Marin Food Bank, 2023). This is coherent with its actions both in the short and long term. In its short-term efforts, the food bank has been setting up a variety of weekly food pantries and delivering groceries daily to those enrolled in its program. This amounts to 55000 households per week. In the long term, the food bank has developed a roadmap with plans to expand and continue being sustainable by spreading awareness of hunger through public advocacy and rigorous testing of pilot programs to increase the efficiency of the organization (San Francisco Marin Food Bank, 2023). Its longevity prompts a look into its structure (San Francisco Marin Food Bank, 2023). An analysis to understand decision-making leads us to map out the network. The nodes are the board members. Edges are information pathways. Though the edges are weighted with a bias towards the board as they decide more influential directions like the adoption of Cruise which I will discuss, the board also respects the opinion of others, and the opinions of organizers and volunteers are considered, each member of the board has a high degree allowing for a high density and high closeness centrality network given their small number. This ensures fast communication and decision-making in the company. In terms of fail-safes, the organization has high robustness as most of the board and high-ranking board members have vice chairs, this allows for increased reachability as issues from organizers on the ground can quickly be voiced and addressed. This high robustness is also seen when most of the board members have been on the company for a decade or more, hinting at stability in values, leadership, and a non-conserved flow of information from the board as the source to the volunteers regarding how the company should be functioning. The network effect of this relatively small organization is a highly efficient network in terms of decision-making. My interactions with the food pop-ups led me to notice that most volunteers are perennial. To map this observation onto the OCEAN model by McCrae & John (1992 shows that these volunteers have a high degree of extraversion and high agreeableness. People with a high degree of extraversion exhibit tendencies to go out and are comfortable with having their time in the company of others. High agreeableness, on the other hand, means that the volunteer has an easy time getting along with others, usually has high levels of empathy, and is less likely to be confrontational. A mix of these two qualities responds to the perennial volunteers having genuine enjoyment of the social interactions between fellow volunteers and the people they help. This allows for the formation of lasting relationships and cohesion of the group in these humble efforts. The board, however, shows a different personality trait: a prevention orientation focus (Shah, Higgins, & Friedman, 1998). This can be seen in their effort to incorporate the food bank’s Policy and Advocacy team which aims to forward new policies to the local government that aligns and help their cause and they’re not afraid to drop projects that aren’t working to avoid further losses (Sleeter, 2023). In addition, having two women with a prevention orientation focus at the head of the board serves well for the company, as Novotney (2023) notes, women leaders are more inspiring and lead to an overall increased productivity in the organization. Together, the board’s focus on sustainable efforts coupled with an enthusiastic volunteer base has proved vital for the organization’s endurance. The legal team also highlights a preference, or rather, a dislike, for risk. Kimball (1993) in his paper describes this as a “loss-aggravating risk,” where there is an exacerbation of negative consequences associated with a reduction of resources. Though any organization has agency, being a non-profit means relying on sponsorship and at times the mercy of the local government must remain vigilant of its resource use. This can also be seen in their roadmap where they experiment with new pilot schemes for 2023 but will drop unsuccessful ones just a year later (OUR STRATEGIC ROADMAP, 2022). The normative theory tells us to expect humans to behave in a way that is subjectively right, as (Schwartz, 1977) notes, altruism is one of these ways, where a person “feels to take specific helping actions.” To input altruism into Rational Choice Theory is to discover altruistic people decide based on “psychic gratification”: where an individual is emotionally satisfied when engaging in selfless actions (Monroe, 1994). In terms of the organization, the legal team, to encourage support, and the strategic roadmap, to discourage risk, points to an application of instrumental rationality: whereby appropriate actions are put in place so that the desired outcome is achieved (Kolodny & Brunero, 2013). Together, we see decisions from the company aim to prolong the company’s mission. A look into the biggest non-profit organizations shows that donations are their greatest source of revenue (List of 100 Largest Nonprofits by Revenue – Nonprofit News Feed, 2022). The SF-Marina food bank imitates this by having its own donation page (San Francisco Marin Food Bank, 2023). The successful organizations can be termed as reference groups. These groups are defined as those whose actions are used by others as a benchmark and are, therefore, influential (Shibutani, 1955). By incorporating the approaches of the bigger companies, SF-Marina hopes to achieve the same sustainable success. The food bank isn’t always conformist as we see that it was one of the first to endorse Cruise (self-driving cars) to support its mission of delivering groceries to those enrolled (Sleeter, 2023). This comes as only 30% of the American public approves of self-driving cars (Brooks, 2023). This action, during the onset of the pandemic, is justified as the manpower was thinning but the need for food was not. The successful integration of the fleet into SF-Marina’s delivery service saw Cruise strike partnerships with similar organizations like Replete (Sleeter, 2023). This shows the capacity of SF-Marina as a minority influencer as through an unorthodox but successful action, they were able to convince others to act in accordance (Wood et. al, 1994). This also shows, though unintended, their role as norm entrepreneurs as they led to increased acceptance of self-driven cars to the extent that other companies adopted them (Sunstein, 2019). The ability to frame new acceptable perspectives by questioning existing social norms, and successfully get others to accept that self-driving cars are actually useful hints at the “framing power” of the food bank (Morrison et al., 2017). SF-Marina recognizes that it doesn’t act in a vacuum. The ability of its legal team to convince the local government to support its cause by passing legislation, for example, successful lobbying at getting free food for all school children in the state is one among four other policies rolled out just this year (Sleeter, 2023). This shows an increasing alignment of interests between the non-profit and the local government. This alignment is defined by Luke (1989), as the third face of power, where SF-Marina is becoming more successful at convincing the local government to support its efforts against hunger in the district. This approach to the local government, as Luke (1989) could be further classified into the first dimension of power (visible power) as they identify “who controls important resources” and then went ahead to make sure “their interests get represented.” SF-Marina recognizes that it cannot advocate for these changes by itself and has teamed up with other non-profits advocating for food equality. In addition, the food bank alerts individuals who are interested in its mission to voice for higher resource allocation from both the country and federal government. This shows the acknowledgment of wealth, to drive initiatives, and numbers, a collective cry is more potent to leaders, especially in a democracy, as sources of power (Are You More Powerful than You Think?, 2017). These decisions have made SF-Marina’s hope of food equality easier to achieve as they can now reduce their allocation to children as the government is feeding them and focus on other communities through the increased grants they now receive. Collectively, I think the underlying greatest achievement could be embedding the concern of food security to the public such that the withdrawal of SF-Marina is inconsequential to the fight, achieving invincible power according to Luke (1989).
