McQuillan and James (2010) state that course completion is significantly influenced by student satisfaction, which gauges the quality of a given educational program. Most of the time, students in postsecondary education programs drop out due to dissatisfaction with their classes. In a positive feedback loop, student satisfaction increases self-confidence, which in turn helps students gain information, acquire practical skills, and become more self-assured.

The overall student satisfaction with the university is influenced by the sum of their experiences (Letcher & Neves, 2010). We can understand students’ satisfaction with online educational systems with the following data analysis.

The dataset used was based on a survey taken during the pandemic. Students were asked how they felt from where: 1 means strongly disagree, 2 means disagree, 3 means neither agree nor disagree, 4 meaning agree, and 5 meaning strongly agree that their level of satisfaction with their current online classes compared to how they felt satisfied with in-person classes. Our dataset alerted us of a discrepancy between the two, with the mean level of satisfaction being 20% less in online classes: a 4.01 in-person compared to a 3.04 online. The in-person satisfaction levels have a higher mean of 4.01, seen through its left-skew and the mode forming a noticeable peak at 4, while online has a lower mean at 3.04, a median of 3, and a mode of 4, showing a cluster of values between 2-4. Figure 2: A histogram with aggregate data of Figure 1 using bins of 1-3 (low satisfaction) and 4-5 (high satisfaction). This mode of the groups shows that most students felt less satisfied when online compared to when they were in person. This hints at an overall drop in satisfaction due to increased technological use impacting students whose professors were less adept at utilizing these tools, impacting the new classroom experience.

The visualizations depicting student satisfaction levels between in-person and online classes suggest a preference for in-person learning experiences. Notably, there is a reduction in extreme dissatisfaction with in-person classes, with very few students selecting the lowest satisfaction level. However, there are significantly more instances of lower satisfaction ratings for online classes, which could point to challenges such as technical difficulties or a lack of personal interaction. The highest level of satisfaction is less frequently reported for online classes, suggesting that while they may be adequate for some educational needs, they potentially need certain elements that drive a higher satisfaction rate. The balance point is at level 3, where students are neither dissatisfied nor highly satisfied with online classes. This visualization of data could imply that, although online classes serve as a functional educational platform, there is a discernible leaning towards the in-person educational experience, and it raises questions about what factors in online learning impact student satisfaction levels.

Our hypothesis is that teachers’ knowledge and skills in using classroom technologies (e.g., Zoom and Google Meets) cause a difference in student engagement, thereby shaping the overall satisfaction with the learning experience. We put forth a causal mechanism explaining how skillfully integrating technology tools and processes improves the quality of education overall, leading to higher satisfaction levels and improved student involvement. Our hypothesis aims to fill a gap by exploring how varied technology strategies influence student satisfaction, providing valuable insights for effective educational practices.

To test the hypothesis, a 6-week interventional study will be conducted to examine the causal mechanism reflected by the hypothesis. As per Roddy et al., 2017, this duration demonstrates the shift in educational institutions to shorter, intensive online study periods. An interventional study design is chosen to determine that the treatment causes the outcome while controlling extraneous variables.

The independent variable, instructor competency, will be measured on a scale from 0-10 based on a competency test that includes practical online tasks that will be scored based on accuracy and efficiency of completion. The dependent variable, student satisfaction, a reliable proxy for ICT initiatives’ success in e-learning (Keržič et al., 2021), will be measured post-study through surveys. Survey questions will focus on the students' satisfaction with their instructor and online learning experience.

Three control groups will be formed based on instructor competency levels: low (0-4), medium (5-7), and high (8-10). All control groups will take the same course and use the same learning materials to ensure consistency across the control groups and minimize the impact of irrelevant variables (e.g. course difficulty, learning style, bias) in the system on the study findings. Randomization will be employed to divide students into these three main groups and then into smaller class-like groups under each instructor. Prior research indicates a moderate link between student satisfaction and grade point averages (Wach et al., 2016). To avoid influencing academic outcomes, especially grades, in an ethically questionable manner, the proposed study will not affect student grades.

One of the strengths of the study method proposed is its relevance to the current world, considering the growing online learning industry. Minimizing the impact of other variables in the system for consistency across controlled groups and investigating the causal mechanism with quantitative variables will ensure accuracy. However, some limitations are that it might only be generalizable to some online classes, considering the impact of factors such as the use of different technologies, courses, preferences, and personalities of students and instructors. Also, awareness of being part of a study might influence students’ and instructors’ behavior, potentially skewing the results (Kenton, 2022).

We assume that the most significant factor in the quality of instruction is the ability of the professor to disseminate information to the student. This means activities like extra discussion and even student preparedness for a course do not influence the quality of instruction. This is validated by (Solinas et al., 2012), who note that the quality of instructors to disseminate information and their encouragement efforts count for students to feel the quality of instruction. In our study, we would control encouragement efforts with the same reward system and leave instructors' ability as the most crucial factor. We also assume that the students will not notice the difference in instruction quality. This is important to avoid feedback loops where students instructed by high-proficiency instructors are more motivated than the students from other groups. This is validated by (Wayne et al., 2013), who note that students in medical school rate their environment more highly and perform better on exams than students who rate their environments lower. We also assume equal technological competency on the students’ part where none of the platforms limit the students; for example, they cannot ask questions and hence are limited in their participation. This is important as students prefer in-person classes as they feel they have immediate access to help compared to online ones (O’Neill & Sai, 2014). Our hypothesis, however, assumes that the lack of a choice meant students had no preferential treatment and would not be a factor.

We expect to observe a positive causal relationship between the independent variable (instructor competency) and the dependent variable (student satisfaction), which is consistent with our hypothesis that instructor competency strongly influences student satisfaction in online learning environments. In particular, we anticipate that, according to the post-study surveys, student satisfaction scores would grow in tandem with increased instructor competency levels. In line with our hypothesis, the three control groups—low, medium, and high instructor competency—would show a distinct slope in student satisfaction. It is anticipated that the students in the high competency group will be the most satisfied, demonstrating the beneficial effects of quality online education. On the other hand, lower satisfaction levels are expected from the low competency group, highlighting the significance of teacher competence in influencing students' opinions about their online learning experience. This finding may contradict our hypothesis that there are no appreciable variations in student satisfaction among the three groups. Given the chosen online course and learning resources, such a result would force reevaluating the presumptive direct relationship between instructor competency and student happiness. It might imply the existence of additional significant elements that our original theory overlooked, calling for more investigation among the students. These expected results advance our general knowledge of the efficiency of online education, especially in light of the expanding online education sector. Whether the results support or refute our original hypothesis, they will provide policymakers and educational institutions with information on the complex relationships between teacher competency and student satisfaction, which will help shape future approaches to online learning.
