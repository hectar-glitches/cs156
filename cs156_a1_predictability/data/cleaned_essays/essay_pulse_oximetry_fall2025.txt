Pulse oximeters estimate arterial oxygen saturation (SpO2 ) using red and infrared light, and their readings often guide urgent clinical decisions. However, recent evidence shows that these devices overestimate oxygenation in individuals with darker skin pigmentation. Sjoding et al. (2020) found that Black patients were nearly three times more likely than White patients to experience blood arterial oxygen overestimation at the same reported SpO2 . Feiner and Hickey (2020) similarly documented reduced accuracy among subjects with darker skin, especially at low saturations. These findings suggest that pigmentation-induced optical effects may bias the measurements produced by standard two-wavelength oximeters. Yet it remains unclear which aspects of device design, calibration, or wavelength selection contribute most strongly to this disparity.

Clinical research consistently shows that pulse oximeters are less accurate in individuals with darker skin pigmentation. Sjoding et al. (2020) demonstrated a substantially higher rate of occult hypoxemia among Black compared with White patients at equivalent reported SpO2 levels. Feiner et al. (2007) observed larger errors across multiple commercial devices in subjects with darker skin. Hickey et al. (2020) reported that these inaccuracies are most pronounced at lower oxygen saturations. These papers indicate that the disparity is reproducible across devices and clinical settings. Historical features of device development may explain this pattern. Early oximeter calibration studies relied on small groups of light-skinned volunteers, as noted in Severinghaus and Honda (1987). Jubran (1999) further notes that the original 660 nm and 940 nm wavelengths were selected to maximize hemoglobin contrast rather than to minimize melanin absorption. As a result, current algorithms implicitly assume optical properties consistent with lighter skin. Melanin’s absorption spectrum provides an explanation for these discrepancies. Bickler et al. (2005) showed that melanin strongly attenuates the 660 nm wavelength, shifting the ratio-of-ratios upward and causing overestimation of SpO2 . Bashkatov et al. (2005) reported that melanin absorbs visible-red light far more strongly than near-infrared light.

Together, these findings suggest that pigmentation acts directly on the optical signal before device noise, fitting errors, or electronic processing occur. Several engineering approaches have been proposed to mitigate pigmentation-related bias. Nitzan et al. (2014) showed that alternative wavelength combinations less affected by melanin could improve signal robustness. Budidha and Kyriacou (2014) tested wavelength sets chosen to reduce melanin interference. Ding et al. (2018) demonstrated that multispectral imaging techniques can separate overlapping chromophore signals, including hemoglobin and melanin. Optical modeling provides additional insight. Liu et al. (2013) used Monte Carlo simulations to show how melanin alters photon transport and PPG amplitude. These simulations reinforce empirical findings that pigmentation affects the measured optical signal. Zhao et al. (2005) introduced early methods for decomposing multi-wavelength PPG signals. Edmonds et al. (2014) expanded these techniques to recover physiological signals in optically complex tissues. Despite these advances, significant gaps remain. Existing studies do not systematically evaluate how varying melanin concentrations distort the ratio-of-ratios. Light–tissue interaction models rarely explore which wavelengths would minimize this effect. Clinical datasets rarely include pigmentation measures or controlled desaturation events. As a result, an understanding of how melanin alters the optical pathway remains incomplete.

This study proceeded in two stages: an exploratory analysis of an existing photoplethysmography (PPG) and oxygen saturation (SpO2 ) dataset, followed by a set of controlled simulations designed to evaluate the effects of melanin concentration and signal noise on pulse oximeter performance. Preliminary Data Analysis We analysed the Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center (BIDMC) PPG/respiratory dataset, which contains raw PPG waveforms sampled at 125 Hz alongside minute-level summaries. After removing non-numeric header rows and harmonising the data formats, we visualised waveform structure and temporal variation in recorded SpO2 .

However, the dataset did not include skin pigmentation or melanin concentration, making it unsuitable for quantifying pigmentation-related bias. Additionally, the SpO2 values demonstrated limited variability and few desaturation events, constraining the ability to study bias across clinically relevant saturation ranges. These limitations motivated the development of a synthetic modelling framework that would allow systematic manipulation of pigmentation, haemoglobin absorption, and noise characteristics. Synthetic Data Generation Pulse oximetry bias is believed to arise from wavelength-dependent absorption of red and infrared light by melanin within the epidermis, which modifies the ratio-of-ratios used to estimate arterial oxygen saturation. Because real-world datasets rarely contain melanin measures and because melanin cannot be manipulated experimentally, simulation provides a principled approach for isolating the contribution of pigmentation to SpO2 estimation error. Synthetic modelling also allows experimental manipulation of noise levels, AC/DC amplitudes, and optical absorption properties that cannot be ethically or practically controlled in clinical settings. Therefore, the purpose of synthetic data in this study is not to reproduce clinical distributions but to model the mechanisms through which pigmentation may influence pulse oximeter performance. We simulated 5,000 synthetic subjects. True SaO2 values were sampled uniformly from 70–100%. Melanin volume fractions were sampled from Beta distributions representing light, medium, and dark pigmentation. DC components were generated from log-normal distributions. AC components were set to 2–5% of DC amplitude and scaled by wavelength-specific reflectance values derived from the optical model. To capture varying measurement conditions, we simulated four proportional noise levels (1%, 2%, 5%, and 10%).

How Melanin Alters the Signals Used in Pulse Oximetry Pulse oximeters estimate oxygen saturation by comparing the AC and DC components of light at two wavelengths. Because clinical datasets cannot experimentally vary pigmentation, they cannot reveal how melanin changes these components. The simulation isolates this effect. Figure 2 shows that increasing melanin concentration causes the baseline signal to decline more steeply at 660 nm than at 940 nm. The pulsatile strength of the red signal, reflected in its AC/DC ratio, shrinks steadily as melanin increases. This imbalance shifts the ratio-of-ratios before any noise or device processing occurs, revealing a core optical mechanism by which pigmentation distorts SpO2 estimation. The 660 nm curve shows steep attenuation with increasing melanin, while the 940 nm curve declines only slightly. This pattern demonstrates melanin’s disproportionately strong absorption at the red wavelength. Melanin-Dependent Bias in SpO2 Estimation Having shown how melanin alters AC/DC ratios, we next examined the resulting estimation bias. Across one million simulated subjects, increasing melanin concentration produced progressively larger ratio-of-ratios values, leading to higher estimated SpO2 even when true saturation was unchanged. Effect of Measurement Noise on Bias Variability We evaluated how measurement noise interacts with pigmentation-dependent bias by simulating four noise levels. Although the spread of the bias distribution increased with noise, the median bias remained nearly unchanged across conditions. Thus, noise worsens precision but does not mitigate or reverse the underlying pigmentation-driven shift. Summary of Findings Melanin disproportionately attenuates the red wavelength, altering AC/DC ratios and shifting the ratio-of-ratios. This shift alone is sufficient to cause overestimation of SpO2 in ideal conditions. Noise widens the error distribution but leaves the fundamental bias intact. Because the BIDMC dataset lacked pigmentation information and substantial desaturation events, these insights required simulation. Future Research The simulation revealed a key causal mechanism that is difficult to observe in clinical datasets: melanin alone can shift the ratio-of-ratios and produce SpO2 overestimation. Future work should validate and extend this finding through improved pigmentation measurement and alternative wavelength evaluation. Empirical studies should replace race-based categories with quantitative measurements such as melanin index. Edwards et al. (2022) showed that spectrophotometric melanin measures predict optical attenuation and bias more accurately than racial labels. Shang et al. (2022) demonstrated that spectrally derived melanometry provides physiologically grounded estimates of melanin concentration. Engineering-focused research should evaluate whether different wavelength combinations can reduce melanin absorption. Liu et al. (2013) showed that wavelengths in the 700–800 nm region experience markedly lower melanin attenuation. Nitzan et al. (2014) demonstrated that incorporating additional wavelengths can enhance robustness. Budidha and Kyriacou (2014) tested alternative LED wavelength sets and found preliminary evidence of reduced melanin interference. Together, these strands of research would enable next-generation oximeters that deliver equitable accuracy across diverse pigmentation levels.
