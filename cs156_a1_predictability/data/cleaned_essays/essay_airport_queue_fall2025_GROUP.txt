Airport security is a queueing system with a distinctive dependency: a small fraction of travelers are flagged for additional screening by a single senior officer, and while a flagged traveler is being cleared, that traveler’s lane is paused. This queue–blocking interaction makes standard M/M/c or textbook M/G/c formulas unreliable on their own.

On the empirical side, we run multiple independent simulations per c, report means and 95% confidence intervals for waiting time and queue length, and compare simulations to two references: a basic no-blocking lower bound and the conservative queue-blocking curve. This paired analysis lets us (a) identify the capacity threshold and (b) evaluate how closely simple formulas predict the simulated system under the assignment’s rules. We also compare against a basic per-lane M/G/1 lower bound that ignores lane pauses.

Our goal is to construct a queuing model that (i) captures the key operational features of the airport security area in the assignment, (ii) matches the mechanisms actually implemented in our simulator, and (iii) is structured to support quantitative prediction and comparison. The modeled system begins when a traveler arrives at the security area and ends when the traveler exits after completing all required screening. Travelers first pass through one of n identical primary screening lanes. Upon completing primary screening, a traveler is either cleared or flagged for additional screening by a single senior officer. Crucially, when any traveler is flagged, the queue service stops until the senior officer completes that screening.

The stochastic primitives are the inter-arrival times Ai , the primary service times S1,i at lanes, and the additional-screening times S2,i at the officer.

On arrival, each traveler is routed by Join-the-Shortest-Queue across c identical first-come-first-served lanes. Each lane serves one traveler at a time. The senior officer is a single FCFS server handling flagged travelers in order.

We implemented a discrete event simulation using a priority queue to manage events chronologically. The main event types are traveler arrivals, service completions, and additional screening completions. This event-driven approach accurately captures the asynchronous nature of airport operations, where multiple stations operate independently. The simulation uses object-oriented design with separate classes for travelers, service stations, the senior officer, and event scheduling. This modular structure simplifies testing and maintenance while clearly separating different system components. Key Implementation Decisions The critical challenge was modeling lane blocking during additional screening. Each service station maintains an is blocked flag that prevents new service while a traveler undergoes additional screening. When a traveler is flagged, their station blocks until the senior officer completes the screening.

Queue lengths are measured as time-weighted averages rather than simple arithmetic means.

To be conservative across all c, we used a uniform 500-minute warm-up for every configuration and computed all metrics on the remaining horizon. Each configuration was run 30 independent replications with different random seeds. We report the sample mean and 95% confidence intervals. This answers the “multiple runs” and “statistical method” requirements in the assignment instruction and keeps the comparisons fair across c.

After removing a 500-minute warm-up from each daily run and averaging over 30 independent replications, the system transitions from overloaded to stable between six and seven stations. The simulation points lie between a no-screening lower bound and a lane-blocking upper bound, which matches the mechanism of occasional lane pauses for additional screening.
