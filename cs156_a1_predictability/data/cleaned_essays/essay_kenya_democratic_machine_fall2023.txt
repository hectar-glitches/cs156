In the modern day, people hail democracy as the bridge of compromise. People admire established democracies as a sign of advancement and appreciate peaceful transitions of power. While many argue this is the case in Kenya, I contend that this system is hurting our sense of national unity. Politicians have realized that the key to a successful election is to motivate their tribespeople, garner enough votes, and form a majority from that support. This tactic can be seen in party formations and tensions that ensue every election cycle. For Kenya to progress economically and be one of the models of inclusive societies, these democratic processes must evolve to prioritize national unity over tribal and regional affiliations as leaders only seek to reimburse the support from their faithful. Kenya has 42 different tribes. The unity within these tribes served as the points on which we based our fight for independence. Movements like the MauMau brought several tribes together who swore by oath to help each other undermine the chokehold of the British occupation. There was increased representation of Kenyans in the government who voiced the concerns of the people and continued to criticize these foreign oppressors to avert further pressure and damage on the colony (A Brief History on Kenya | KENYA Embassy of the Republic of Kenya in Japan, 2022). The formation of tribal alliances alerts us that our ‘struggle for independence’ set a precedent of unity. However, since our independence in 1963 with over 12 election cycles, you’d be surprised if I tell you less than five tribes have held the top seat. It’s even worse. Only two have done so. These two tribes, Kikuyu and Kalenjin, hold a significant advantage over the rest: they are the largest ethnic groups in the country. The other two most prominent groups: Luo and Luhya, are almost as big but don’t suffer from the same success as they hardly vote in the same font. In Kenya’s electoral process, you only need the majority to win. Kenya’s prep-up to national elections frequently involves new party formations. Unsurprisingly, politicians change between coalitions as they try to get as many tribes as possible under these umbrellas. The frequent formation of new parties and shifting of political alliances in Kenya, as noted by Elischer (2018), aptly illustrates a political landscape dominated by 'ethnic coalitions of convenience' rather than genuine ideological alignments. This pattern, where politicians hop between coalitions to amass tribal support, reflects a deep-rooted challenge in Kenya's democratic process. It highlights the tactical use of ethnic identities for political gain, as seen in the strategic alliances within KANU, our first party, to secure electoral dominance with just the prominent ethnicities. The hypocrisy was evident when we had a referendum implemented in 2010. This referendum altered the political unit of the country from provinces to smaller counties, a devolved government. Without hesitation, politicians did their ethnic whizz, and new convenience parties formed. This political wit, as Bunde & Otieno (2023) note, reveals how the northeast region was lagging in terms of economic growth as they lacked the votes to swing an election. Only with the referendum introducing the county system were leaders compelled to incorporate representatives from the northeast region into their alliances did the northeast finally catch up with the national growth rate. Such a political climate characterized by fluid party structures and a focus on ethnic arithmetic, as Elischer describes, undermines the development of a stable, issue-driven party system and hinders long-term national development. My high school Swahili teacher recollected his experience of the 2007 election crisis. Being a Kikuyu living in a region populated by Luhya, his granaries were torched by those he considered friends. Kagwanja & Southall (2009) note the sequence of events: the election results were out, the incumbent had won, “robbed of victory,” cried the opposition, a series of jabs between the incumbent, a Kikuyu, and the opponent, a Luo, ensued. The report continues by describing the harsh rhetoric summed up by “41 against 1,” which led to widespread violence and persecution of Kikuyus, which was only stopped by a power-sharing agreement proposed by the UN and the African Union. The report reminds us that this wasn’t the first as the previous elections, though of less destruction, had the same flow. How can Kenya claim that this democratic process is of any benefit when lives are on the line every five years? The suspicion only reinforces ethnic misunderstanding and complicates economic integration. When trust among different ethnic groups is compromised, it hampers collaborative ventures and mutual economic initiatives, essential for a nation's growth. Businesses suffer as entrepreneurs and consumers alike become cautious in environments perceived as unstable or hostile. This suspicion transcends into missed opportunities for cross-tribal economic partnerships, stunting innovation and limiting market expansion. Moreover, such a climate of distrust can deter foreign investment, as investors typically seek stable and harmonious regions for their ventures. The economic cost of ethnic division, therefore, extends beyond immediate material losses from conflict—it hinders the overall trajectory of national economic progress and prosperity. How can we improve? Appiah discusses in his book, “The Lies That Bind: Rethinking Identity,” how the Singaporean direction after independence might offer hope. In pursuing national unity with its own tribal identities, Kenya can draw valuable lessons from Singapore's CIMO model and Sedition Act, tailoring these policies to its unique cultural context. The structured recognition of diversity in the CIMO model offers a blueprint for Kenya to acknowledge and respect its 42 tribes formally. Ethnic recognition will ensure equitable representation and access to governance, education, and cultural policies. This approach promises integration without forcing each Kenyan to allegiance, without which they cannot expect development. Integration will, hopefully, make Kenyans look beyond tribes and into policy. Furthermore, politicians must deliver as they can’t back “their people” to provide support. This will make it more challenging, as inspired by Appiah, to create an us-vs-them narrative from the Robert Cove experiment as political identities are not shaped by names (in Kenya, the middle names are predominantly ethnically based) nor region as CIMO nurtured interethnic interaction by equal allotment of public housing. Simultaneously, adapting aspects of Singapore's Sedition Act could help Kenya legislate against hate speech and tribal incitement. The Act is particularly crucial during election periods as it puts a high price on divisive rhetoric. The fact that this high bar extends to everyone helps grow a culture of tolerance, mutual respect, and unity. By embracing these strategies, imperfect though they may be, Kenya can forge a future where its diversity is not a fault line but a foundation for a stronger, more unified nation. Some might argue that my case only serves to dilute our ethnic richness. By focusing on a national ideological society, we might loose what makes us unique: our ethnic diversity. Elections have become moments to remind us of what we value. Though harsh rhetoric stands out, politicians dig deep to find what matters to the people. Becoming an established leader within a tribe requires finesse to outclass other leaders from the grassroots. A tribe's attachment to a leader rises because they resonate with their base. The word representative couldn’t be a better fit. To this, I ask, where is the line drawn? Leaders should be able to resonate beyond their ethical backgrounds. If Kenya is to be heard or even advance for her voice to be heard on the global stage, leadership should not be confined to tribal allegiance but should extend to embodying a vision that is inclusive and representative of all Kenyans. This calls for a redefinition of political success — not as an ascent within ethnic silos but as the ability to unite diverse voices in a shared narrative of growth and prosperity. In doing so, Kenya can preserve its unique ethnic mosaic while building a cohesive and powerful presence on the world stage, showcasing how diversity can be a unifying force rather than a divisive one. This exploration into Kenya's political landscape, deeply influenced by tribal affiliations, reveals an interplay between historical context, contemporary electoral dynamics, and the intricate fabric of tribal identities. The essay underscores that while tribal affiliations have been central to political representation and advocacy, they have also fostered divisions that challenge the cohesion and economic progress of the nation. Moving forward, Kenyans must consider strategies that maintain the rich diversity of tribal cultures while forging a stronger, more inclusive national identity. Inclusion and recognition call for a national dialogue aimed at reconciling tribal differences and reimagining political structures to promote unity. By doing so, Kenya can aspire to a democratic model that not only respects its ethnic diversity but also harnesses it as a strength, setting a precedent for other nations grappling with similar challenges.
